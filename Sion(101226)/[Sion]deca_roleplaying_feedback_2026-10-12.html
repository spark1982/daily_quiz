Event Situation

You are to assume the role of small-business loan officer at Prairie Ridge Community Bank in Lawrence, Kansas. Your job is to review loan requests and help local owners understand their finances.

Dana Morales owns Golden Crust Bakery, which has two locations. She wants to borrow $120,000 to open a third shop near the university. Her latest statements show: current assets of $90,000 and current liabilities of $60,000; total debt of $150,000 and owner’s equity of $100,000; net sales of $600,000 and net income of $30,000. Last year her net profit margin was 8%, but the price of flour and butter has risen sharply. Dana says she does not understand what these numbers mean and only wants to know whether the bank will lend her the money.

Your manager has asked you to explain to Dana what her current ratio, debt-to-equity ratio, and net profit margin show about the bakery, and to recommend what she should do before or while expanding.

The bakery owner (judge) will begin the role-play by asking you, “So, is my bakery healthy enough for a loan?”

Judge's Question

“If I could only watch one of these numbers over the next year, which one should it be, and why?”

Task: In 150–250 words, write what you would say to the judge. Address all four performance indicators and answer the judge's question.

Your answer

Thank you for consulting me! Right now, unfortunately, I don't think your bakery is ready for this loan. Let me explain why the numbers say so. All of these numbers we see are part of what we call a financial statement. In simple terms, a financial statement is like a report card for a company. It tells you how the company is doing financially. Firstly, we know that your current assets are $90,000 and your current liabilities are $60,000. Essentially, your "assets" are things your company owns with value, meaning that they could converted to cash, sold, used up, etc. One example could be your inventory. The bread you sell has value and is an asset. Your liabilities, on the other hand, are obligations that your company needs to pay. These are usually things you owe, like salaries you have to pay for your employees. The "current" versions of these two measurements: current assets, and current liabilities, help us because they can be used to calculate the current ratio. That is just the ratio of your current assets over current liabilities. If this ratio is above 1.0, then you have more assets than liabilities, which is generally a good thing: you could comfortably cover your short-term bills. If the ratio is below 1.0, it's the opposite. In your case, your current ratio is 90,000/60,000 which is 1.5! However, this only applies to the short term. The "current" assets and liabilities only consider assets and liabilities that could be sold or need to be paid within a year or other cycle time. That's why we also need to look at the total debt and owner's equity as well. Total debt is somewhat similar to current liabilities; however, it is the total debt the company has rather than the debt in the current moment. Owner's equity, on the other hand, is net worth of the company: the total value of all the assets it has. Owner's equity and total debt are quite similar to current assets and current liabilities respectively, but applying to the whole company over all time rather than just in the current moment. The ratio of the total debt to owner's equity is your debt-to-equity ratio. However, since this ratio has total debt as the numerator, it is to be interpreted a bit differently. Your debt-to-equity ratio is 150,000/100,000, also 1.5! However, this actually means that our debt is larger than our equity. Specifically, for every $1.00 of equity we have, we have $1.50 of debt. This means that your company is heavily borrowing to fuel growth, but this gives us high risk of default if business slows or interest rates rise. So far, this tells you that right now you can comfortably cover your short-term costs, but overall you have significant debt that could put you at risk if you cannot repay it. To determine if you can repay this debt, we need to look at how much you are actually earning. We determine this with three more numbers: net sales, net income, and net profit margin. Fortunately, these are relatively simple concepts. Net sales is simply how much money a company has made from what they do, or revenue. For your company, that would be selling baked goods. Net income, on the other hand, is how much of the net sales your company actually keeps for profit. Net income is net sales after you pay off all your expenses, like rent, salaries, etc. Net profit margin is just that percentage: what percentage of net sales is net income. For your case specifically, the issue is that your net sales are $600,000, but your net income is only $30,000. You have a net profit margin of 8%. This is a little low. This alone wouldn't a huge issue, but if you combine this with the 1.5 debt-to-equity ratio, this does become a bit of a concern. To summarize, your current ratio is 1.5, which means that right now you can comfortably cover costs, but your debt-to-equity ratio is also 1.5, which means that you have considerable debt that needs to be paid off in the long term. However, your net profit margin is only 8%, so business is a little slow. This combined with the fact that the price of flour and butter has sharply increased recently suggests that there is real risk that your bakery may struggle to pay off its existing debt, and likely isn't ready to take on even more. What I would recommend before trying to expand would be to trying to conquer the debt you have already. In the current moment, you can comfortably cover short term costs with some to spare, $30,000 to be exact. I'd recommend you reinvest and save as much of this as possible. Perhaps you could reinvest about $5,000 into marketing, and another $10,000 into savings. This leaves $15,000 still left, while giving some effort to try to increase/sustain revenue and have cash ready to try to pay off debt when necessary. If you wanted to look at only one number over the next year, I would recommend the debt-to-equity ratio. This tells you how much total debt you have compared to equity, which can be a strong indicator on how healthy your company is and how well it is at handling debt. I believe that this would be the most important to consider before trying to take on new debt. Thank you again for consulting me, and please do not hesitate to contact me again later if you have any more questions.

What to improve

  • Fix the definition of equity. Instead of “Owner’s equity... is net worth of the company: the total value of all the assets it has,” try: “Owner’s equity is what the bakery would be worth to you after paying off all its debts: assets minus liabilities.”
  • Link each action to a problem. Instead of “Perhaps you could reinvest about $5,000 into marketing,” try: “Because your margin fell, raise prices on your top three items by 25 cents and compare two flour suppliers before you borrow more.”

Model answer

Dana, thank you for coming in. Right now, I would not approve a $120,000 loan, but your bakery has real strengths, and I can show you how to get ready.

Your financial statements are like a report card. The balance sheet shows what you own and owe, and the income statement shows your sales, costs, and profit. Together they tell a lender if you can repay.

First, your current ratio is $90,000 ÷ $60,000 = 1.5. You can pay this year’s bills and still have $30,000 of working capital. That’s good.

Second, your debt-to-equity ratio is $150,000 ÷ $100,000 = 1.5, so you already owe $1.50 for every dollar you own. Another $120,000 would raise that to 2.7, which is risky.

Third, your profit margin fell from 8% last year to $30,000 ÷ $600,000 = 5% this year because flour and butter cost more.

My advice: raise prices slightly on your best sellers, compare flour suppliers, and use part of your profit to pay down debt. When your margin is back near 8% and your debt-to-equity ratio is near 1.0, let’s talk again about a smaller loan.

If you watch one number, watch your net profit margin each month. It shows right away whether rising costs are eating your profit.
