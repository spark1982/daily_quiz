Event Situation

You are to assume the role of business analyst at Lakeshore Community Bank, a local bank with nine branches in and around Milwaukee, Wisconsin.

The bank’s Elmwood branch opened 210 new checking accounts from July through September of last year, but only 147 during the same three months this year, a drop of 30 percent. The branch manager believes customers have simply lost interest in checking accounts. However, the bank’s records also show the following for this year’s third quarter compared with last year’s: lobby visits stayed about the same (roughly 4,100 per month); the number of tellers on the floor during lunch hours was cut from four to two in June; the average wait time in the lobby rose from 6 minutes to 14 minutes; and a customer survey found that 38 percent of visitors left the branch without finishing what they came to do. Over the same period, the bank’s other eight branches saw new checking accounts rise by an average of 4 percent.

The regional operations manager (judge) has asked you to explain why the bank should look at data before deciding what caused the drop, interpret what the numbers above suggest, recommend key performance indicators the Elmwood branch should track each week, and explain how you would present your findings clearly to the bank’s leadership team.

The regional operations manager (judge) will begin the role-play by greeting you and asking for your analysis. After you have presented your ideas and answered the manager’s question, the manager will conclude the role-play by thanking you for your work.

Judge’s follow-up question

If you could track only one number every week to know whether your plan is working at the Elmwood branch, which number would it be, and why?

Task: In 200–600 words, write what you would say to the judge. Address all four performance indicators. The judge will ask you one follow-up question when you finish.

Your answer

Hello, thank you for consulting me. To pinpoint what actually caused this drop in checking accounts, it is essential to ensure that we have all of the information necessary before making business decisions. It is extremely difficult to make accurate conclusions if you don't have enough information. New information can challenge existing assumptions and promote new, often more accurate ones. In our case, we noticed a drop from 210 to 147 checking accounts from July through September. However, the actual number of people walking into our lobbies did not decrease: it stayed quite constant from last year to this year at around 4,100 people a month. What has changed from then is that the number of tellers on the floor during lunch hours has decreased from four to two. At the same time, the average wait time in the lobby rose from 6 to 14 minutes, and 38% of visitors could not finish what they came in to do by the time they left. With this new information, we can reasonably assume that the reduction in the number of tellers caused visitors to have to wait longer to get their tasks done and have less success in doing those tasks. This likely led to frustration from these visitors. In fact, the final piece to this is that other branches in this bank have noticed about a 4% increase in their checking accounts. Our visitors did not lose interest in checking accounts, but rather migrated to other branches because they were more efficient and successful. This shows how these descriptive statistics are essential for decision-making. On their own, each of these numbers and statistics don't tell us much. However, when we interpret them in their context, we can draw connections and more accurate conclusions. We can deduce that people didn't lose interest in checking accounts because the number of people visiting our lobbies did not decrease, but rather the amount of people who could effectively get one did. These statistics are key performance indicators that can tell us how our bank is performing! To ensure that we can avoid this kind of event again, we can track a few key performance indicators each week. The ones that I would recommend include the number of visitors physically stepping into our lobbies, the number of tellers we physically have available over the work hours, how long the visitors have to wait, and how often a visitor can get what they came in to get done done. That way, we can measure the ratio of how many people want service to how well we can provide service to how well people are receiving service. These performance indicators can paint a pretty strong image of how well our company is doing at the moment. Now, to present these results to our leadership team, it is essential that we are clear and understandable. These raw numbers and statistics mean nothing to decision-makers: they can't make a reasonable decision off of numbers without knowing what they mean. That's why we need to ensure we not only say the numbers, but what they actually mean, and how they are important to business decisions. In our case, we can ensure that we connect how consistent lobby visiting means that demand is still strong, but lower teller availability, longer wait times, and less task success are likely pushing visitors to other branches. We need to ensure that they walk out knowing not the numbers 6, 14, or 38%, but rather that our checking accounts are fewer not because demand is lower, but because we are providing them less efficiently.

Judge’s follow-up question

If you could track only one number every week to know whether your plan is working at the Elmwood branch, which number would it be, and why?

Your answer to the judge

If I had to pick only one number, I would pick the percentage of people successfully doing what they came in to do in our lobby. Ideally, we want to keep this percentage as high as possible. Satisfied clients are usually clients that could get what they wanted to get done, done, and satisfied clients usually tend to stick around. Thank you again for consulting me, and please feel free to contact me again if you have any more questions.

What you did well

  • You let the data overturn the assumption. “The actual number of people walking into our lobbies did not decrease” immediately shows why the manager’s guess is wrong. That is exactly what this PI asks for.
  • You connected the numbers into a cause-and-effect chain. Fewer tellers → longer waits → unfinished visits → customers going to other branches. Using the other branches’ +4% as a comparison was especially smart.
  • You knew the real message for leaders. “Not because demand is lower, but because we are providing them less efficiently” is a headline a busy executive will remember.

What to improve

  • Recommend an action. Your analysis points straight at the fix, but you never say it. Add one sentence: “As a first step, I recommend restoring two lunch-hour tellers by staggering breaks, and checking our KPIs after one month.” Judges often reward the solution as much as the diagnosis.
  • Give each KPI a target and include the outcome. Instead of “how long the visitors have to wait,” say “average wait time, target under 8 minutes.” Also track new checking accounts per week — that is the result the bank cares about.
  • Say how you will present the data. You explained why clear presentation matters but not how. Try: “I’d show one before-and-after chart of wait time and new accounts, with the main finding as the title.”
  • Use the timing clue and careful wording. Mention that the teller cut happened in June, just before the drop. And say the data suggests customers moved to other branches — the numbers show it is likely, not certain.
  • Trim repeats. “Essential” appears three times, and “get done done” reads like a typo. Shorter sentences leave room for the action plan above.

What the judge was looking for

  1. Need for information: say that decisions should be based on facts, not guesses, and show how the facts here change the decision (a marketing fix vs. a staffing fix).
  2. Descriptive statistics: read each number in context (steady visits, wait time doubled, 38% left unfinished, other branches +4%) and state what they suggest together, without overclaiming.
  3. Key performance indicators: define a KPI as a measurable value linked to a goal, and pick a few weekly KPIs with targets, including the outcome (new accounts).
  4. Presenting data clearly: lead with the main finding, use one simple visual, avoid raw number dumps, and end with a recommendation leaders can approve.

Model answer

Good afternoon, and thank you for asking me to look into the Elmwood branch. Before we decide what caused the drop, we need the full picture. Decisions based on a guess can send money in the wrong direction. If we assume customers lost interest, we might pay for a new checking-account ad campaign, and that would not fix the real problem. Good information lets us test our assumptions before we spend. Here is what the numbers tell us. New checking accounts fell 30 percent, from 210 to 147. But lobby visits stayed steady at about 4,100 a month, so customers are still coming in. What changed is service. In June, lunch-hour tellers were cut from four to two. Right after that, the average wait more than doubled, from 6 to 14 minutes, and 38 percent of visitors left without finishing what they came to do. Meanwhile, our other eight branches grew new accounts by 4 percent. Together, these statistics suggest that demand is still there, but customers are not being served, and some may be opening accounts at other branches instead. To manage this, I recommend that Elmwood track a few key performance indicators each week. A KPI is a measurable number tied to a goal. I suggest four: new checking accounts opened, with a target of 16 a week to match last year; average lobby wait, under 8 minutes; the share of visitors who complete their task, above 90 percent; and tellers on the floor at peak hours. As a first step, I would restore two lunch-hour tellers by staggering breaks for one quarter and then check the KPIs. Finally, for leadership, I would keep it simple: one chart showing wait time and new accounts before and after June, and a one-page summary that starts with the main finding. The message is that the drop is a service problem we can fix, not a lack of interest.

Answer to the judge: I would track the percentage of visitors who finish what they came to do. It shows whether our service fix is working, and it leads directly to new accounts. If it climbs from about 62 percent back above 90 percent, I would expect new checking accounts to recover too.
