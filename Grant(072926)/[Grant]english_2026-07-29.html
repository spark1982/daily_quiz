Master of Disguise: How the Octopus Changes Color

Imagine walking past a rock on the seafloor and having no idea that the rock is actually a living animal, watching you with two clever eyes. Octopuses are famous for this kind of trick. Within a fraction of a second, an octopus can shift its skin from mottled brown to bright red to a pale, sandy tan, blending almost perfectly into whatever surface it happens to be resting on. Scientists have long been fascinated by how an animal without color vision, as many octopuses appear to have, can match its surroundings so precisely.

The secret lies in thousands of tiny structures called chromatophores embedded in the octopus's skin. Each chromatophore contains a small sac of pigment surrounded by a ring of muscles. When the octopus's brain sends a signal, those muscles contract, stretching the sac wide and revealing more color, or relax, letting the sac shrink and fade. Because chromatophores are controlled directly by the nervous system rather than by hormones, an octopus can change its appearance almost instantly, all while chasing prey, hiding from a predator, or apparently even expressing something like a mood.

Color is only part of the disguise. Beneath the chromatophores, an octopus has muscles that can raise small bumps, called papillae, on its skin, allowing it to mimic the rough texture of coral or the ridges of a rock. Some species, such as the aptly named mimic octopus, take this skill even further, contorting their arms and bodies to imitate the shape and movement of other sea creatures, including flatfish, sea snakes, and lionfish, apparently to confuse or frighten away predators.

Researchers studying this remarkable system hope it might one day inspire new materials, such as flexible screens or adaptive camouflage for military use, that could change color and texture the way octopus skin does. For now, though, the octopus remains one of the ocean's most astonishing performers, a soft-bodied animal capable of vanishing into its surroundings in the blink of an eye.
