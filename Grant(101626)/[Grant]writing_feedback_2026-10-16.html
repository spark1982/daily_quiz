The topic

Topic

Should every classroom have a class pet?

Type of writing

Opinion — Tell what you think and give reasons.

How to write it

  1. Say your opinion in your first sentence: should every classroom have a class pet, or not?
  2. Give your first reason and an example (for example, what kids would learn or what could go wrong).
  3. Give your second reason and an example.
  4. End with a sentence that says your opinion again in a new way.

Sentence starter

“I think every classroom should (or should not) have a class pet because ___.” (You can use this or write your own first sentence.)

What you did great

  • Your opinion is crystal clear. “I think every classroom should not have a class pet” tells the reader exactly where you stand from the first word. That is the most important job of an opinion paragraph.
  • You gave examples, not just reasons. “a student looking at the pt and trying to make it look at him or her” and “the pet barking” help the reader picture the problem. Examples like these make an opinion convincing.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your paragraph stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar mistakes are in their own section below.)

  1. 1. Split your long first sentence into a reason and an example. Your first sentence holds your opinion, your first reason, and your example — 45 words! A reader runs out of breath. Give the opinion and reason one sentence, then start a new sentence for the example with “For example.”

    Before: “…not pay attention to the teacher such as a student looking at the pt and trying to make it look at him or her.”
    After: “…stop listening to the teacher. For example, a student might stare at the pet and try to make it look at him or her.”

  2. 2. Don’t start a new reason in your last sentence. Your plan said to end by saying your opinion again in a new way. Your last sentence brings in a brand-new reason (the pet could escape), so the paragraph stops suddenly instead of finishing. Move the escape idea up as a reason, and write a real ending sentence.

    Before: “Every classroom shouldn’t have class pets because they can try to escape and run away.” (last sentence)
    After: “…a pet could also try to escape and run away, and then the class would spend its time chasing it instead of learning. A classroom is a busy place for learning, so a pet should live in a gentle, quiet home instead.”

  3. 3. Use “distract” less often. You used distracted, distract, and distracting three times, plus “pay attention to the teacher” twice. Saying the same words again makes your two reasons sound like one reason. Try other exact words: interrupt, stop listening, stare at.

    Before: “loud noises that can distract the students trying to pay attention to the teacher such as the pet barking or any noises and distracting the teacher and students.”
    After: “loud noises, such as barking or squeaking, that interrupt the whole class.”

  4. 4. Replace “any noises” with a real example. “Barking or any noises” is vague — and most class pets (hamsters, fish, birds) don’t bark! Name a sound that class pets really make.

    Before: “such as the pet barking or any noises”
    After: “such as barking or squeaking”

  5. 5. Answer the other side with “however.” Strong opinion writers show they know what the other side thinks, then explain why they still disagree. Many people say a class pet teaches kids to be responsible. Mention that, then use however to turn back to your side. (This also uses two of your bonus words!)

    Before: (no sentence about the other side)
    After: “Some people say a pet would teach kids to be responsible; however, a pet could also try to escape and run away…”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Spelling: “pt” should be “pet.” A letter went missing. Read your paragraph one more time before you press Submit — small typos like this jump out when you read slowly.

    Before: “looking at the pt”
    After: “looking at the pet”

  2. 2. “Such as” goes right after the thing it gives examples of. Such as means “like these examples,” so it has to sit next to a word like noises. In your sentence it comes after “the teacher,” so it sounds like the teacher is the example. Put a comma before it and place it right after the thing you are giving examples of — or start a new sentence with “For example.”

    Before: “the pet might make loud noises that can distract the students trying to pay attention to the teacher such as the pet barking”
    After: “the pet might make loud noises, such as barking or squeaking, that interrupt the whole class.”

  3. 3. Fix the run-on ending of your second sentence. “…or any noises and distracting the teacher and students” adds an -ing phrase that doesn’t connect to anything, so the sentence just keeps going. End the sentence once your example is finished.

    Before: “such as the pet barking or any noises and distracting the teacher and students.”
    After: “such as barking or squeaking, that interrupt the whole class.”

  4. 4. Match “pets” and “they.” Your first sentence talks about a class pet (one), but the last sentence says class pets and they (more than one). Pick one and stick with it. Since each classroom would have one pet, use a pet and it.

    Before: “Every classroom shouldn’t have class pets because they can try to escape”
    After: “a pet could also try to escape”

Your sentences all start with capital letters and end with periods, and you used the apostrophe in shouldn’t correctly. Nice!

Did you use the three words?

  • however — Not this time. However is perfect for answering the other side: “Some people say…; however, …” (See tip 5.)
  • responsible — Not this time. It fits when you mention what the other side thinks: “a pet would teach kids to be responsible.”
  • gentle — Not this time. Try it in your ending: “a pet should live in a gentle, quiet home.”
