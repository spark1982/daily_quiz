The topic

Topic

You discover a map that leads to a hidden treasure in your neighborhood.

Type of writing

Narrative — Tell a story with a beginning, middle, and end.

How to write it

  1. Beginning: tell who you are, where you find the map, and what it looks like.
  2. Middle: follow the map step by step — tell two places it leads you in your neighborhood (like a park, a big tree, or a mailbox).
  3. Tell one problem that happens on the way and how you solve it.
  4. End: tell what the treasure turns out to be and how you feel.

Sentence starter

“I was cleaning out the garage when a folded, yellow piece of paper fell out of an old box.” (You can use this or write your own first sentence.)

Try to use these words

finally  ·  mysterious  ·  clue

What you did great

  • Your clue system is really smart. “The color of the paper was the color of the mysterious item.” A yellow paper leads to a yellow spot, a red paper leads to the red car — that is the kind of idea that makes a reader want to keep going.
  • You showed what was going on in your head. “I was curious, so I decided to follow it” and “The red piece of paper had to be a clue” let the reader think along with you. Good storytellers do this!

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your story stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar mistakes are in their own section below.)

  1. 1. Tell the reader it is a map. Your topic was about a treasure map, but your paragraph never says the word map. You call it “a folded yellow piece of paper” and then “it.” The reader needs to know it is a map so they understand why you follow it. This is also a great place for your word mysterious.

    Before: “My name is Grant, and it was crumpled up like it had lasted for many years.”
    After: “It was a mysterious map, and it was so crumpled that it looked many years old.”

  2. 2. Keep each sentence about one idea. “My name is Grant” and “it was crumpled up” are two ideas that don’t go together, so joining them with and sounds strange. In a story told with “I,” the reader already knows you are the main character, so you can leave your name out.

    Before: “My name is Grant, and it was crumpled up…”
    After: “It was a mysterious map…”

  3. 3. Help the reader picture the yellow spot. A hole in the ground is brown, so “a yellow hole” is hard to picture. Your idea is clever — each paper’s color tells you where to go next — so show what was yellow at that spot.

    Before: “I found a yellow hole in the ground, which had a red piece of paper.”
    After: “There, I found a hole in the ground with a yellow flag next to it, and inside the hole was a red piece of paper.”

  4. 4. Add the problem the plan asked for. The plan said: “Tell one problem that happens on the way and how you solve it.” A problem makes the middle of a story exciting. You already had a perfect spot for one — figuring out what the red paper meant! I added a new sentence there.

    Before: “(no problem in the story)”
    After: “At first, I could not figure it out, but then I remembered that our car is red.”

  5. 5. Explain your great color idea in one clear sentence. “I started to understand” is a good moment, but the reader has to wait for the next sentence to learn what you understood. Join them with a colon (:) so the “aha!” comes right away. Also, “the mysterious item” is vague — say what the color matched: the next hiding place.

    Before: “I started to understand. The color of the paper was the color of the mysterious item.”
    After: “I started to understand: the color of each paper matched the color of the next hiding place.”

  6. 6. Make the ending clear: where, what, and how you feel. “Inside was a paper” — inside what? The reader needs to know you looked inside the car. Then the ending, “found that I got a new car,” comes very fast. The plan asked you to tell what the treasure turns out to be and how you feel. Slow down, show the surprise, and use your word finally.

    Before: “Inside was a paper that said, “look inside the house” so I did, and found that I got a new car.”
    After: “Inside the car was a note that said, “Look inside the house,” so I did. Finally, I found Mom and Dad holding a set of keys, and they told me our family had a brand-new car! I felt so excited that I jumped up and down.”

  7. 7. Choose exact words. “It made me go to” sounds like the paper forced you. Maps and clues lead you places. And “like it had lasted for many years” is close, but looked many years old says it more clearly.

    Before: “So I followed it and it made me go to my red car.”
    After: “I followed the clue, and it led me to the red car.”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Start a quote with a capital letter, and put the comma inside the quotation marks. The words someone (or a note) says are like a new sentence, so the first word gets a capital letter. When the sentence keeps going after the quote, put a comma before the closing quotation mark.

    Before: “Inside was a paper that said, “look inside the house” so I did…”
    After: “Inside the car was a note that said, “Look inside the house,” so I did.”

  2. 2. Use a comma before and when it joins two complete sentences. “I followed it” and “it made me go to my red car” could each be a sentence by itself. When and joins two of them, put a comma before and.

    Before: “So I followed it and it made me go to my red car.”
    After: “I followed the clue, and it led me to the red car.”

Everything else is spelled correctly, and you put a comma after “First” and before “so” in “I was curious, so I decided” — nice!

Did you use the three words?

  • finally — No — this one was missing. A good place is right before the big surprise: “Finally, I found Mom and Dad holding a set of keys.”
  • mysterious — Yes! “The color of the mysterious item.” Next time, use it to describe the map itself: “a mysterious map.”
  • clue — Yes! “The red piece of paper had to be a clue.” Great spot for it.
