The topic

Topic

You invent a robot helper. What does it do?

Type of writing

Narrative — Tell a story with a beginning, middle, and end.

How to write it

  1. Beginning: Tell who you are, where you are, and how you built your robot. Give it a name.
  2. Middle: Tell what happens when the robot starts helping. Does something go wrong, or does it surprise you?
  3. End: Tell how the story ends and how you feel about your robot helper.
  4. Use time words like One morning, Then, and At last to move the story along.

Try to use these words

Suddenly  ·  clever  ·  whirred

Task: Write one paragraph of at least 100 words (aim for 100–150). Use the plan above and try to use the three words.

What you did great

  • Your story has a problem and a fix. “Suddenly, it got every question wrong, but the next day I rearranged it” — that is the exciting middle the plan asked for. Something goes wrong, and your character solves it.
  • You gave your robot a fun name and a reason for it. “It was named Grant jr. because it’s like mini me” makes the reader smile and helps them picture the robot.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your story stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar is in its own section below.)

  1. 1. Start with who you are and how you built the robot. A story’s beginning should set the scene first. Your first sentence jumps to a problem (“I couldn’t think of something for it to do”) before we even know who you are. Move your name and the building part to the front, then tell the problem.

    Before: “I invented a robot helper, but I couldn’t think of something for it to do. My name is Grant, and I used a technique that my dad taught me.”
    After: “My name is Grant, and one morning I built a robot helper in my garage using a technique my dad taught me. … At first, I couldn’t think of a job for it.”

  2. 2. Say what the “technique” or tools were, or where you built it. The plan asked where you are. Adding one place (a garage, your room) and one detail makes the beginning easy to picture.

    Before: “I used a technique that my dad taught me.”
    After: “one morning I built a robot helper in my garage using a technique my dad taught me.”

  3. 3. Break the long idea sentence into two clear sentences. When one sentence holds too many ideas joined by and, the reader gets lost. Put the “clever idea” in its own sentence and use a colon (:) to show what the idea was.

    Before: “It was very clever, and I thought of something to do and it was for it to do my study and transfer the information to my brain.”
    After: “Then I had a clever idea: Grant Jr. would do my studying and transfer the information to my brain.”

  4. 4. Split the “Suddenly” part so the surprise stands out. “Suddenly” is a great word for a surprise, so give the surprise its own short sentence. Then explain the fix in the next sentence. Also say how you rearranged it (its wires, its buttons).

    Before: “Suddenly, it got every question wrong, but the next day I rearranged it, and it got most of the questions right so it wouldn’t be suspicious.”
    After: “Suddenly, it got every practice question wrong! The next day, I rearranged its wires, and it got most of the questions right, but not all of them, so no one would get suspicious.”

  5. 5. End with how you feel. The plan said: “Tell how the story ends and how you feel about your robot helper.” Your story stops right after the test, so the reader doesn’t know your feelings. One more sentence that starts with At last gives the story a real ending. A strong ending can even make the reader think — for example, wondering if you really learned anything yourself.

    Before: “(no ending sentence)”
    After: “At last, I realized I had the most helpful robot in the world, but I also wondered if I was really learning anything myself.”

Grammar fixes

Every grammar, spelling, capital-letter, and punctuation fix, one by one.

  1. 1. Capitalize “Jr.” “Jr.” is short for Junior and is part of a name, so it starts with a capital letter.

    Before: “It was named Grant jr.”
    After: “I named it Grant Jr.”

  2. 2. Keep the story in the past tense. Your story happens in the past (invented, was), so “it’s like” (it is) should be “it was like.” Also add a before “mini me.”

    Before: “because it’s like mini me”
    After: “because it was like a mini me”

  3. 3. Use “studying,” not “study.” After “do my,” you need the -ing word for the activity: do my homework, do my studying.

    Before: “to do my study”
    After: “do my studying”

  4. 4. Add a comma before a long “and” part. Your last sentence runs two actions together. A comma before and shows where the second action starts.

    Before: “Then it whirred and did its job every day and on the test at school, the robot gave its information to me”
    After: “From then on, it whirred quietly every night and did its job, and on the test at school, it sent the information to me”

Did you use the three words?

  • Suddenly — Yes! “Suddenly, it got every question wrong” — perfect for a surprise.
  • clever — Yes! “It was very clever.” Next time try using it to describe the idea: “I had a clever idea.”
  • whirred — Yes! “Then it whirred” — a great sound word for a robot.

At last, I realized I had the most helpful robot in the world, but I also wondered if I was really learning anything myself.
