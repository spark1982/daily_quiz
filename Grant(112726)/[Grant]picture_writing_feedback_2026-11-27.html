What you did great

  • You described the main action. “The dog started shaking its wet fur around back and forth really fast” — that is exactly what is happening in the photo, and “back and forth” helps the reader see it.
  • You made a smart guess about what happened before and after. “The dog probably went into it while the woman was sitting with her feet in it” and “the dog might jump in the pool again” show you are thinking like a detective, using clues in the picture.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your description stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar is in its own section below.)

  1. 1. Use present tense to describe a picture. When you describe what you see in a picture, write as if it is happening right now: is shaking, is laughing. Use past tense only for what happened before the picture (the dog jumped in).

    Before: “the dog started shaking its wet fur … and the woman thought it was very playful.”
    After: “it climbed out and started shaking its wet fur … The woman is laughing because the dog is being so playful”

  2. 2. Describe the people and the dog with more details. The plan asked for describing words (colors, sounds, sizes, feelings). Tell what the dog and the woman look like — small, fluffy, smiling — so the reader can picture them.

    Before: “There is a woman and a dog”
    After: “A smiling woman and a small, fluffy dog”

  3. 3. Show the splash. The best part of the picture is the water flying off the dog. Say what the water does — that makes your writing come alive.

    Before: “shaking its wet fur around back and forth really fast”
    After: “shaking its wet fur back and forth so fast that drops of water flew everywhere”

  4. 4. Don’t say the same idea twice. You wrote that the woman thought the dog was “very playful” in two sentences in a row. Say it once, and add a new detail instead (she looks happy to get splashed).

    Before: “and the woman thought it was very playful. The woman probably thought the dog was very playful and was very happy to see it.”
    After: “The woman is laughing because the dog is being so playful, and she looks happy to get splashed.”

  5. 5. Use exact words instead of “went in” and “rounds.” Strong verbs like jumped, leap, and dangling paint a clearer picture than went in. “Rounds” is for games or boxing; for repeating something, say “times.”

    Before: “the dog went in … a few more rounds”
    After: “The dog must have jumped into the pool … a few more times”

Grammar fixes

Every grammar, spelling, capital-letter, and punctuation fix, one by one.

  1. 1. “to” should be “do.” This is a spelling slip: you meant the action word do.

    Before: “and to the exact same thing”
    After: “and do the exact same thing”

  2. 2. Fix the run-on sentence. Your third sentence joins three complete sentences with only commas. That is called a run-on. Split it into separate sentences, or join them with a word like and or so.

    Before: “But just then the dog went in and got soaked, the dog started shaking its wet fur around back and forth really fast, and the woman thought it was very playful.”
    After: “The dog must have jumped into the pool and gotten completely soaked. Just then, it climbed out and started shaking its wet fur back and forth so fast that drops of water flew everywhere.”

  3. 3. Put a comma after “Just then.” When a time phrase starts a sentence, put a comma after it. Also, try not to start a sentence with “But” when it doesn’t show a change.

    Before: “But just then the dog went in”
    After: “Just then, it climbed out”

Did you use the three words?

  • Just then — Yes! “just then the dog went in” — next time start the sentence with it and add a comma: “Just then, …”
  • soaked — Yes! “got soaked” is exactly right for a dripping dog.
  • playful — Yes! You used it twice — once is enough.
