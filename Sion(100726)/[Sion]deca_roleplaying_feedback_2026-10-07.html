Event Situation

You are to assume the role of operations assistant at Ridgeline Bike Works, a bicycle shop in Boulder, Colorado, that sells about 60 bikes a month and runs a busy repair center. The shop orders bikes from one manufacturer in Taiwan. Since spring, shipping delays have stretched the manufacturer’s lead time from 4 weeks to 11 weeks. Twice this year the shop ran out of its best-selling commuter bike, and the store manager estimates the shop lost about 35 sales, or roughly $24,000 in revenue.

To avoid running out again, the owner wants to place one huge order of 400 bikes right now. That order would cost $180,000, use almost all of the shop’s cash, and fill the storage room so full that repair parts would have to be kept in the hallway. Meanwhile, repair customers already wait an average of 6 days because mechanics spend time searching for parts. The owner has asked you to recommend a better way to manage bike inventory and suppliers and to speed up the repair center.

The owner (judge) will begin the role-play by asking for your recommendations in the shop’s back office.

Judge's Question

“If we order fewer bikes at a time, how will we know exactly when to reorder so we don’t run out again?”

Task: In 150–250 words, write what you would say to the judge. Address all four performance indicators and answer the judge's question.

Your answer

Hello! Thank you for consulting me. Unfortunately, I would not recommend placing a single order of 400 bikes right now. That could potentially cause an even larger shipping delay along with a few other issues like worsening wait times for our repair customers. Instead, I think we need to prioritize improving our inventory control. The nature of inventory control is essentially ensuring that we have the "right" amount of an item in stock. If we have too much, it takes up space without generating revenue, and if we have too little, we miss out on sales. To ensure efficient inventory control, our suppliers are essential to our operation's success. In our case, we have been experiencing longer lead times. These lead times are the times we have to wait after sending an order to our supplier before we can actually receive. Longer lead times make it more difficult to keep the "right" amount of inventory, because if we run out of an item, we may stay out of it for a while if the item takes long to get shipped. Timing when to order from our supplier is very important. For our case specifically, in order to maximize our operational efficiency, I think we should track when our sales for the best-selling bike are highest. We recorded that two times in the year we ran out, so those times are likely when sales are highest. We can plan accordingly. We can order fewer at a time: more as the high-selling seasons approach and less after they end, taking into account the lead times and ordering that amount of time earlier. That leaves the storage room's status more predictable, which allows us to organize our repair tools accordingly. If you need anything else, you can always shoot me an email! Thank you!

What you did well

  • You gave a clear recommendation right away. “I would not recommend placing a single order of 400 bikes right now” tells the owner your position in the first lines, just as a judge expects.
  • Your definitions were accurate and easy to follow. Explaining inventory control as “the ‘right’ amount” and lead time as the wait “before we can actually receive” shows you understand the core ideas.
  • You used clues in the case. Noticing that the shop ran out twice, and that those times probably match busy seasons, is the kind of reasoning that leads to a smarter ordering plan.

What to improve

  • Answer the judge’s question with an exact rule. Instead of “we can plan accordingly,” try: “We will reorder whenever stock drops to our reorder point: weekly sales times the 11-week lead time, plus a small safety stock.”
  • Use the numbers in the case. The $180,000 cost, the 11-week lead time, the 35 lost sales ($24,000), and the 6-day repair wait are all evidence. Try: “A 400-bike order would use $180,000, almost all our cash, to solve a problem that cost us $24,000.”
  • Explain the supplier’s role and add a backup. Instead of “our suppliers are essential,” try: “Our supplier decides whether bikes arrive on time. I’ll ask for scheduled monthly shipments and line up a U.S. distributor as a backup for our best seller.”
  • Solve the repair-center problem directly. The owner asked you to speed up repairs. Try: “We’ll set up a labeled parts area so mechanics stop searching, with a goal of cutting the wait from 6 days to 3.”
  • Keep it near 250 words and stay professional. Cut the opening (“That could potentially cause…”) to one sentence, and replace “you can always shoot me an email!” with “I’m happy to put this plan in writing for you.”

What the judge was looking for

  • PI 1: Inventory control balances the cost of holding too much stock (cash and space tied up) against the cost of running out (lost sales and customers). Here, that means rejecting the $180,000 order without risking more stockouts.
  • PI 2: Suppliers affect delivery time, quality, and cost. A strong answer talks about communicating with the manufacturer, agreeing on scheduled shipments, and having a backup supplier.
  • PI 3: Lead time is the wait between ordering and receiving. Because it grew from 4 to 11 weeks, orders must be placed much earlier, and the reorder point must be higher.
  • PI 4: Operational efficiency means doing the work with less wasted time. Organizing parts so mechanics stop searching directly attacks the 6-day repair wait.
  • Judge’s question: A reorder point: (average sales per week × lead time in weeks) + safety stock. When inventory falls to that number, place the next order.

Model answer

Thank you for asking me. I recommend that we not place one 400-bike order. It would use $180,000, almost all of our cash, and push repair parts into the hallway, slowing repairs even more. Instead, let’s control inventory: keep the right number of bikes, not too many and not too few.

Our biggest risk is lead time, the wait between ordering and receiving. It has grown from 4 weeks to 11, so we must order almost three months ahead. To answer your question, we will use a reorder point. If we sell about 6 commuter bikes a week, we sell about 66 during an 11-week wait. Adding a safety stock of 12 bikes, we reorder whenever our stock drops to 78. I’ll track it weekly on a simple spreadsheet.

Suppliers are part of our success, so I’ll ask our Taiwan manufacturer for scheduled monthly shipments and set up a backup U.S. distributor for the commuter bike.

Finally, to cut repair waits from 6 days, we’ll create a labeled parts area that bikes never fill. Mechanics will find parts in seconds, not minutes.

Example numbers: the case says the shop sells about 60 bikes a month in total; the model assumes about 6 of the best-selling commuter bikes a week.
