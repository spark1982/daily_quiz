Resolved: Justice requires the repatriation of cultural artifacts to their countries of origin. (NSDA 2026–27 Lincoln-Douglas topic)

Your side

You are on the Negative side.

Your opponent argues

“Think about a carved bronze mask that soldiers took from a West African palace in 1897. Today it sits behind glass in a museum in Boston, thousands of miles from the people whose ancestors made it. In this debate, there are two extreme positions. Some museum directors say they should keep every object they own, no matter how it was taken. On the other side, some activists say museums should return every artifact and also pay millions of dollars in damages. Both sides are clearly too extreme. The fair and reasonable answer must lie in the middle: museums should send the artifacts back, but they should not have to pay anything more. Because this compromise sits right between the two extremes, it is obviously the just solution. That is why justice requires the repatriation of cultural artifacts to their countries of origin.”

Task: In 150–250 words: (1) name the flaw in your opponent's reasoning and explain why it weakens their argument, and (2) give your own rebuttal with at least one reason or example that supports your side.

Your answer

Judge, the fact that an action sits in the middle of two extremes does NOT automatically make it the perfect choice. The affirmative has not connected their action to the resolution. They have not truly told us why "justice requires the repatriation of cultural artifacts to their countries of origin." The only thing they have said is that their action sits between two extremes. They have provided no real reason why museums should return these artifacts back. At face value, returning artifacts to the original people whose ancestors created them seems like a good idea. However, you must take into account every aspect. Artifacts displayed in museums are incredibly valuable to scientists, archaeologists, researchers, and the many people generally interested in history and its artifacts. By simply returning all of them, you are taking away the magical opportunity for people outside of the indigenous groups to experience the window into history that artifacts provide. Justice does not require the return of cultural artifacts to their countries of origin, because you are taking away a magical piece of a history from everyone else. Artifacts are special gateways that allow the general public to see a past they did not get to live. There are little, if any, other methods of getting such an experience. It is a lot closer to injustice to take away such an experience from all these people.

The flaw in the argument

Middle Ground Fallacy — assuming that a compromise between two positions must be correct just because it lies in the middle. The opponent described two “extremes” and then claimed their plan was “obviously the just solution” only because it “sits right between” them. Where a plan sits says nothing about whether it is right.

Model answer

Judge, my opponent’s whole case rests on one claim: their plan is just because it sits between two extremes. That is the middle ground fallacy. A position is not fair simply because it lies in the middle; if one side said “steal the mask” and the other said “protect it,” the halfway point would not be justice. My opponent never explains why returning artifacts is right, only where their plan sits. Without that reason, they have not proven the resolution.

Now, why justice does not require repatriation in every case. First, justice must weigh everyone affected, including the millions of people who learn from these objects. The British Museum welcomes about six million visitors a year, many of whom will never travel to the artifacts’ home countries. Second, there are better solutions than a single rule. When an object was clearly stolen, like the mask in my opponent’s example, it can be returned through a case-by-case review. But many artifacts were bought or traded legally, and some are safer in museums with strong conservation labs. Long-term loans and shared exhibits can honor origin communities while keeping history open to everyone.

Because my opponent offers only a midpoint, not a reason, and because justice is better served case by case, please vote Negative.
