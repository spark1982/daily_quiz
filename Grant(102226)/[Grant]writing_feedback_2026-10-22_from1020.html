The topic

Topic

Explain how to save money for something you want.

Type of writing

Informative — Teach the reader about something, step by step.

How to write it

  1. Introduce the topic: tell the reader you will explain how to save money for something you really want.
  2. Explain the steps in order: First, Next, Then, Finally (pick a goal, find out the price, put money away, keep track).
  3. Add one tip that makes saving easier (for example, a jar with a picture of your goal, or waiting a day before buying something small).
  4. End with a sentence that tells how it feels to finally buy the thing you saved for.

Sentence starter

“If you want to buy something special, you can save money for it by following a few simple steps.” (You can use this or write your own first sentence.)

Try to use these words

first  ·  goal  ·  patient

What you did great

  • You used order words like a pro. “First,” “Next,” “Then,” and “Finally” each start a new step, so the reader always knows where they are. That is exactly what informative writing needs.
  • You gave honest, real-life advice. “It won’t happen tomorrow… You have to be patient.” Telling the reader what is hard about saving makes your paragraph sound like it comes from someone who really knows.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your paragraph stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar mistakes are in their own section below.)

  1. 1. Pick the goal first. The plan said your first step should be to pick a goal and find out the price. You did write about goals — “You can use goals like $100, $200” — but that sentence comes near the very end, after you have already bought the thing! Move the goal to the front, and make it one real thing with a real price. Then every step after it makes sense.

    Before: “First, start by having a way to earn money.”
    After: “First, pick a goal, like a $60 skateboard, and find out exactly how much it costs.”

  2. 2. Tell how to earn the money. “Having a way to earn money” and “keep doing that until you have money” are true, but they don’t teach the reader anything new. Informative writing gives specific examples so the reader can actually do it.

    Before: “Next, you have to keep doing that until you have money.”
    After: “Next, find a way to earn money, such as doing extra chores or selling lemonade. Keep earning until you reach your goal.”

  3. 3. Explain how to keep track. Your step says “keep track of your money,” but not how. Tell the reader exactly what to do. Also, “well then” sounds like talking out loud. In writing, give the reason clearly instead.

    Before: “If you lose it, well then it is gone.”
    After: “Keep it in a safe place, because if you lose it, it is gone.”

  4. 4. Add the saving tip from the plan. The plan asked for one tip that makes saving easier. A tip is the “secret trick” that makes your paragraph more helpful than other people’s. I added a new sentence for this.

    Before: “(no tip)”
    After: “One tip is to tape a picture of your goal to your money jar so you remember why you are saving.”

  5. 5. Make the ending tell how it feels. After “Finally,” your paragraph keeps going with another “Then.” Finally should be the last step. The plan also asked you to end by telling how it feels to buy the thing you saved for. “Have fun with your item” is close, but item is a vague word — say how it feels and why.

    Before: “Then you can go home or go somewhere else and have fun with your item.”
    After: “It feels great to finally have it, because you earned it yourself.”

  6. 6. Say each idea once. “You can gradually earn money in a way that most people like” repeats the earning step and doesn’t tell the reader anything new (which way do most people like?). Because the goal moved to the start and the earning ideas are already in step 2, this sentence became part of those sentences.

    Before: “You can use goals like $100, $200, and you can gradually earn money in a way that most people like.”
    After: “First, pick a goal, like a $60 skateboard, and find out exactly how much it costs.”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Spelling: few, not dew. Dew is the water on grass in the morning. You meant few, which means “not many.” It’s probably a typo — the d key is right next to f! Reread your first sentence before you submit.

    Before: “by following a dew simple steps”
    After: “by following a few simple steps”

  2. 2. Don’t join two sentences with only a comma. “It won’t happen tomorrow” and “earning money takes time” are both complete sentences. A comma alone can’t hold them together (that’s called a comma splice). Add a joining word like because, or use a period.

    Before: “It won’t happen tomorrow, earning money takes time.”
    After: “It won’t happen tomorrow, because earning money takes time.”

  3. 3. Put a comma after well. When well is used like a pause word at the start of a thought, it needs a comma after it: “well, then.” (In the polished version this sentence changed, but remember the rule!)

    Before: “If you lose it, well then it is gone.”
    After: “If you lose it, well, then it is gone.”

  4. 4. Use first or start, not both. “First, start by” says the same thing twice, because first already means “start.” Pick one.

    Before: “First, start by having a way to earn money.”
    After: “First, pick a goal…”

Everything else is spelled correctly, and you put commas after “First,” “Next,” and “Then” — nice!
