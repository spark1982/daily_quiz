The Boston Molasses Flood

On January 15, 1919, in Boston's North End neighborhood, a strange disaster struck without warning. A massive steel tank, five stories tall, held more than two million gallons of thick molasses meant for making rum and industrial alcohol. The tank had been built quickly two years earlier, riveted together in a rush to meet wartime demand. Workers who built it never tested it properly, and neighbors sometimes complained that it groaned and leaked syrup onto the street.

That afternoon, the temperature had risen sharply from near freezing to about forty degrees Fahrenheit, and the molasses inside the tank had been fermenting, building pressure. Around half past noon, the tank suddenly burst apart with a deep rumbling roar that people said sounded like a passing train. A wave of molasses, reportedly as high as a two story building, surged through the streets at an estimated thirty five miles per hour. It swept away wagons, crushed a firehouse, knocked buildings off their foundations, and trapped horses and people in its sticky grip. Twenty one people died, some drowned in the syrup and others crushed by debris, and about a hundred and fifty more were injured.

Rescue crews worked for days, wading through molasses that thickened as it cooled, making every movement exhausting. Cleanup crews used salt water from the harbor and sand to cut through the syrup, and the cleanup dragged on for weeks. In the years that followed, more than a hundred lawsuits were filed against the company that owned the tank. An investigation eventually found that the tank's steel walls were too thin and had been poorly riveted, meaning the structure was never strong enough to hold its contents safely. Some residents of the North End claimed for years afterward that on hot summer days, they could still catch a faint whiff of molasses drifting from the pavement, though scientists say this is almost certainly just a local legend passed down through generations.
