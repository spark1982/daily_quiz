The flaw in the argument

Anecdotal Evidence — using a few personal stories as if they prove something about a whole group. The opponent’s case rested on one neighbor’s knee injury in Canada, one cousin’s sore throat in England, and their own family’s experience. Three stories cannot show how a health system works for millions of people.

Model answer

Judge, my opponent’s entire case rests on two stories: a neighbor who waited for a knee specialist and a cousin who waited three hours for a sore throat. This is the anecdotal evidence fallacy. Two personal experiences cannot tell us how a health system treats millions of people, and my opponent offers no study showing that these waits are typical. Even their own example, a family that sees a doctor within a week, is just one more anecdote.

Now look at what the Negative leaves out. Notice that both people in my opponent’s stories still received care. Today, about 26 million Americans have no health insurance at all, and many of them skip care entirely because of the cost. A slower appointment is a problem; no appointment is a much bigger one. National health insurance would cover every American. It would also lower costs: when the government is the main buyer of care, it can negotiate prices with hospitals and drug companies, the same way large buyers get discounts in any market. That is why researchers at the Yale School of Public Health estimated that a single-payer system could save money and many lives each year.

A system that works for my opponent’s family does not work for the families who cannot pay for it. Because the Negative offers only stories while national health insurance would cover everyone and control costs, please vote Affirmative.
