Event Situation

You are to assume the role of financial assistant at Rolling Thunder Tacos, a family-owned food truck business in Minneapolis, Minnesota, that runs two trucks. From May through September, the trucks are busy every day and the business earns about $38,000 a month in sales. From December through February, cold weather keeps many customers away, and monthly sales fall to about $11,000. Monthly costs, including payroll, food, fuel, and a $1,400 truck loan payment, stay at about $16,000 all year.

This summer was the business’s best ever, and the owner spent most of the extra money right away on new signs and a larger freezer. Now it is October, the business has only $5,000 in its bank account, and a refrigeration unit in one truck needs a $6,500 repair. The owner plans to put the repair on a business credit card that charges 24% interest and to “figure out the winter later.” He has asked you to explain whether this is a good plan and how the business should manage its money through the slow season.

The owner (judge) will begin the role-play by asking for your advice at the food truck’s parking lot office.

Judge's Question

“Next summer, if we have another great season, how much of the extra money should we set aside, and how would we decide?”

Task: In 150–250 words, write what you would say to the judge. Address all four performance indicators and answer the judge's question.

Your answer

Thank you for consulting me! To begin, I think we should look over the nature of our cash flows. You know that we usually earn $38,000 a month in our strong months and about $11,000 a month in the colder ones. That is the revenue we bring in: a positive cash flow. However, as you know, running these trucks is not free. Generally, the cost of keeping this business running is quite consistent throughout the year: a cost of $16,000 a month. This is a negative cash flow. The issue we have, is that in our lower months, we do not bring enough cash in to cover the maintenance costs during the colder months. I believe that its likely not a good idea to put our necessary repair on the business credit card. Business credit is very useful and important for situations like the one we are in, but is it not a good idea to "figure out the winter later". This business card has a 24% interest rate, which gives us debt that grows exponentially, which will become even harder to pay off since we are approaching our coldest months, where sales are lowest. To get us through this month, I recommend we take a larger, standard loan with a lower interest rate if possible. I would suggest a loan of about $25,000 while seeking the lowest rate we can get. There are 3 months (December to February) of "rough season" that usually brings in revenue about $5,000 short of maintenance costs each month, which adds up to a $15,000 deficit. Accounting for the $6,500 repair we need, $25,000 covers these costs and gives us a little extra leg room for the rest. We must ensure we are budgeting this properly, because it has an essential role in business finances. While our cash is tight, we must ensure that we know where every dollar is moving. Even slight discrepancies can add up to huge costs later if they keep recurring. After we get through this season and get back to our time of stronger sales, we must ensure we start an emergency fund to not allow this type of situation to reoccur. Of the $38,000 we bring in these months we take out $16,000 for maintenance costs. That leaves $22,000 still. I recommend we set aside $7,500 out each month immediately after, and lower that to $5,000 after we have fully paid off our loan. $5,000 over the 5 months of strong sales leaves us with an $25,000 for our slower ones. $15,000 of that covers our deficits, and the remaining $10,000 keeps us afloat during the months in between, and whatever remains can be put into savings just in case anything drastic happens. This will ensure that we always have a backup when things fail. An emergency fund is essential to keeping us alive when things are slow sometimes. Thank you for contacting me, and you can always call me again later if you have any more questions.

What the judge was looking for

  • PI 1: Cash flow is the timing of money in and money out. A profitable year can still run out of cash in winter, so the business must plan for the months when costs are larger than sales.
  • PI 2: Business credit helps cover short-term gaps and emergencies, but its cost matters. Compare the 24% card with a lower-rate line of credit or bank loan, borrow only what is needed, and protect the business’s credit history.
  • PI 3: A budget plans each month’s sales and costs, shows the winter shortfall ahead of time, and guides spending choices, such as not spending the summer surplus on signs and a freezer right away.
  • PI 4: An emergency cash reserve pays for surprises (like a $6,500 refrigeration repair) without expensive debt, and should be built during the busy season.

Model answer

Thank you for asking. I don’t recommend putting the repair on the 24% credit card, because that debt will grow during the months when our cash flow is weakest. Cash flow is the timing of money coming in and going out. We earn $38,000 a month in summer, but only $11,000 in winter, while our costs stay at $16,000. That means we lose about $5,000 a month from December to February, or $15,000 in total. Business credit is useful for gaps like this, but we should choose the cheapest kind. I suggest a small bank line of credit at a lower rate: we borrow only what we need for the repair and the winter, and repay it in May. Next, we need a winter budget. Running one truck instead of two, ordering less food, and booking indoor catering could cut our gap to about $2,000 a month. Finally, this shows why we need an emergency cash reserve: a surprise repair should never force us into expensive debt. To answer your question, next summer we should add up the expected winter shortfall, about $15,000, plus one month of costs, $16,000, as a cushion. Saving that $31,000 over five busy months means setting aside about $6,200 each month before we spend on anything new.
