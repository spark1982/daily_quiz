The Great Emu War

In 1932, farmers in Western Australia faced an unusual enemy: about twenty thousand emus. These large, flightless birds had migrated inland after breeding season, and they discovered that newly cleared wheat fields offered easy meals. The emus trampled fences, ate crops, and left gaps that let rabbits stream in to finish off whatever remained. Veterans who had fought in World War I, many of them struggling financially during the Great Depression, pleaded with the government for help.

The Australian Defence Ministry agreed to send Major G.P.W. Meredith and two soldiers armed with Lewis machine guns, convinced that a quick military operation would settle the matter. Meredith expected an easy victory. He was wrong. When the men opened fire on their first flock, the emus scattered in every direction, running in unpredictable zigzags that made them almost impossible to hit. "They can face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks," Meredith later admitted, comparing the birds' resilience to armored vehicles.

Over the following weeks, the soldiers pursued emu flocks across the countryside, mounting a machine gun on a truck at one point, only to watch the birds outrun the vehicle over rough terrain. By the time the campaign ended, thousands of rounds of ammunition had been fired, yet only a small fraction of the emu population had been killed. Newspapers mocked the operation, and one member of parliament joked that the emus deserved medals for their tactical superiority.

Farmers eventually solved their emu problem not with weapons but with fences. The government funded a bounty system and helped build barriers that kept emus away from valuable cropland, a far less dramatic but ultimately more effective solution. Today, the so-called Emu War is remembered less as a military failure and more as a reminder that determination alone cannot defeat an enemy that refuses to fight by predictable rules.
