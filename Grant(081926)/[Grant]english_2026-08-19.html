The lighthouse on Gull Point had stood empty for eleven years, its lamp dark and its stairs choked with dust. Mara had heard the stories from her grandfather: how the light used to guide fishing boats safely past the rocks, and how it had gone dark the winter he retired. She never expected to be the one to climb those stairs again.

It began with a storm, the kind that rattled windows and turned the harbor into a churn of gray foam. Mara's father had gone out early that morning to check the crab traps, promising to return before the clouds rolled in. By noon, the sky had turned the color of a bruise, and he still had not come back.

Mara stood on the dock, gripping the rail so hard her knuckles ached. She could see nothing past the first line of waves. Her grandfather, leaning heavily on his cane, appeared beside her and squinted toward the lighthouse. "If he's lost in that fog, he'll need a light to steer by," he said quietly. "The old lamp still has oil in it, if the wick hasn't rotted through."

Mara ran. She took the lighthouse stairs two at a time, her lungs burning by the time she reached the lantern room. The lamp was coated in grime, and for a terrible moment she thought her grandfather was wrong, that the wick had crumbled to nothing. But when she struck the match and touched it to the wick, a small flame caught, then steadied, then grew until it threw a warm circle of light across the churning water.

She kept the lamp burning for three hours, feeding it oil from a rusted can she found in the corner, refusing to let it go out even as her arms trembled with exhaustion. When her father's boat finally rounded the point, guided by the beam she had lit, Mara realized the lighthouse was no longer just a story her grandfather told. It was hers now, too.
