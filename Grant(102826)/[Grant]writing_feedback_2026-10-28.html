The topic

Topic

Explain the rules of your favorite game or sport to a friend who has never played.

Type of writing

Informative — Teach the reader about something, step by step.

How to write it

  1. Introduce the game or sport: what is it called, and how many people play?
  2. Explain the main rules in order. Use First, Next, Then, and Finally.
  3. Tell how a player wins (or how the game ends).
  4. End with a closing sentence, like a tip for a new player or why the game is fun.

Sentence starter

“If you have never played soccer, here is how the game works.” (You can use this or write your own first sentence.)

Your writing

If you have never played soccer, here is how the game works. Many people play, it can be a 1v1, 2v2, 5v5, it can be any people on each team. What you did great

  • Lots of real information. You told the reader how to score, why passing matters (“it is very hard to score a goal by yourself”), what each position does, and the hand rule. That is exactly what an informative paragraph should do.
  • Time-order words with commas. You used “First,” “Next,” “Then,” and “Finally,” just like the plan asked, with a comma after each one. And you started with the sentence starter to hook a new player.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your paragraph stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar is in its own section below.)

  1. 1. Put every rule before Finally. Finally tells the reader you are at the last step. But after “Finally, whoever has the most goals wins,” you added one more rule about hands. Move that rule up with the other rules, and let Finally be about how the game ends. This is also the perfect place for your missing word, allowed!

    Before: “Finally, whoever has the most goals wins. You also can not touch the ball with you hands, only the goalie can use their hands.”
    After: “Also, you are not allowed to touch the ball with your hands, but the goalie is allowed to use their hands. Finally, when the game ends, the team with the most goals wins.”

  2. 2. Split the long sentence about positions into two. Your sentence about offenders, defenders, and the goalie has great information, but it is 40 words long and hard to follow. First tell the reader what is coming (“learn the positions”), then list the three jobs in one clear sentence.

    Before: “Then, you have offenders, where they go across the field to score, defenders, who stay back and block the ball from going into their goal, and the goalie who is at the net trying to stop the ball.”
    After: “Then, learn the positions. Forwards move up the field to score, defenders stay back to block the ball from going into their own goal, and the goalie guards the net.”

  3. 3. Use the real soccer word. In soccer, the players who try to score are called forwards (or attackers), not offenders. An offender is someone who breaks a rule — the opposite of what you mean!

    Before: “you have offenders, where they go across the field to score”
    After: “Forwards move up the field to score”

  4. 4. Make the second sentence clear and exact. “Many people play” and “any people on each team” are a little fuzzy. Tell the reader exactly what they need to know: two teams play, and a team can be small or large. Writing “1 against 1” is clearer than “1v1” for someone who has never played.

    Before: “Many people play, it can be a 1v1, 2v2, 5v5, it can be any people on each team.”
    After: “Two teams play against each other, and each team can have any number of players, from 1 against 1 to 11 against 11.”

  5. 5. Say it in fewer words. “Score into the net on the other team’s goal” says the same thing twice (the net is the goal). Tell the reader the action: kick the ball in.

    Before: “First, you must try to score into the net on the other team’s goal.”
    After: “First, your team tries to kick the ball into the other team's goal.”

  6. 6. Don’t repeat the same words in one sentence. “Score a goal” appears twice in one sentence, and “pass it” makes the reader guess what it is. Say the ball, and end with why passing matters.

    Before: “Next, it is very hard to score a goal by yourself, so you should pass it to your teammates to eventually score a goal.”
    After: “Next, remember that it is very hard to score by yourself, so pass the ball to your teammates and work together.”

  7. 7. Add a closing sentence. The plan asked you to end with a tip for a new player or why the game is fun. Right now the paragraph stops on a rule. One last sentence wraps it up.

    Before: “(no closing sentence)”
    After: “Soccer is a great game for new players because everyone on the team gets to help.”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Don’t join two sentences with only a comma. “Many people play” and “it can be a 1v1” are each complete sentences. A comma alone cannot hold them together. Use a period, or add a joining word like and.

    Before: “Many people play, it can be a 1v1, 2v2, 5v5, it can be any people on each team.”
    After: “Two teams play against each other, and each team can have any number of players.”

  2. 2. Same rule in your last sentence. “You also can not touch the ball with you hands” and “only the goalie can use their hands” are two complete sentences. Join them with but, or use a period.

    Before: “You also can not touch the ball with you hands, only the goalie can use their hands.”
    After: “Also, you are not allowed to touch the ball with your hands, but the goalie is allowed to use their hands.”

  3. 3. Your, not you. To show that the hands belong to you, write your. (You is the person; your shows something belongs to you.)

    Before: “with you hands”
    After: “with your hands”

  4. 4. Cannot is one word. When you mean “not able to,” write cannot as one word (or say are not allowed to).

    Before: “You also can not touch the ball”
    After: “You also cannot touch the ball”

  5. 5. Use a comma before who when it adds extra information. “Who is at the net” adds extra information about the goalie (there is only one goalie on each team), so put a comma before who.

    Before: “and the goalie who is at the net trying to stop the ball.”
    After: “and the goalie, who is at the net trying to stop the ball.”

  6. 6. Say any number of people. “Any people” is not how we say this in English. When you are talking about how many, say any number of.

    Before: “it can be any people on each team.”
    After: “each team can have any number of players.”

All of your words are spelled correctly, and every time word has its comma — nice!

Did you use the three words?

  • first — Yes! “First, you must try to score…”
  • goal — Yes! You used it several times, like “whoever has the most goals wins.”
  • allowed — No — this one was missing. It fits the hand rule perfectly: “You are not allowed to touch the ball with your hands.”

Your writing, polished

If you have never played soccer, here is how the game works. Two teams play against each other, and each team can have any number of players, from 1 against 1 to 11 against 11. First, your team tries to kick the ball into the other team’s goal. Next, remember that it is very hard to score by yourself, so pass the ball to your teammates and work together. Then, learn the positions. Forwards move up the field to score, defenders stay back to block the ball from going into their own goal, and the goalie guards the net. Also, you are not allowed to touch the ball with your hands, but the goalie is allowed to use their hands. Finally, when the game ends, the team with the most goals wins. Soccer is a great game for new players because everyone on the team gets to help.

Try this next time

When you write a how-to paragraph, make a quick list of the steps before you start, and save Finally for the very last one. If you think of a new rule later, slide it in before Finally.
