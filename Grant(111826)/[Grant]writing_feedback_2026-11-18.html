The topic

Topic

A day when something went wrong but ended well.

Type of writing

Narrative — Tell a story with a beginning, middle, and end.

How to write it

  1. Tell who was there and where you were.
  2. Tell what went wrong. Use time words like First and Then.
  3. Tell what you did to fix it or how it got better.
  4. End by telling how the day ended and what you learned.

Try to use these words

however  ·  disappointed  ·  relieved

Task: Write one paragraph (60–120 words). Use the plan above and try to use the three words.

Your writing

When it was my birthday, me and Caleb were going to Main Event but then a huge storm came and we were very disappointed. My name is Grant, and my friend Caleb was going to a place called Main Event. What you did great

  • You showed how your feelings changed. At the start you were “very disappointed”, and at the end you “were relieved that we still got to play”. That change is the heart of a good “something went wrong” story.
  • You ended with a lesson. “I learned that no matter what happens, it can’t ruin fun” answers the last step of the plan: tell what you learned.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your story stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar is in its own section below.)

  1. 1. Tell who and where first, all in one place. Step 1 of the plan was “tell who was there and where you were.” Your story told about Main Event in the first sentence, and then told it again in the second sentence. Put the who and where together at the very beginning, and say it only once. You also don’t need “My name is Grant” — in a story about yourself, I already tells the reader it is you.

    Before: “When it was my birthday, me and Caleb were going to Main Event …” “My name is Grant, and my friend Caleb was going to a place called Main Event.”
    After: “On my birthday, my friend Caleb and I were going to a place called Main Event.”

  2. 2. Give the problem its own sentence. The storm is the most important moment in your story, but it was hidden at the end of a long first sentence. A short sentence makes a big moment stand out. Start it with the time word Then, just like the plan asked.

    Before: “… but then a huge storm came and we were very disappointed.”
    After: “Then a huge storm came, and we were very disappointed.”

  3. 3. Say the “storm can’t ruin fun” idea only once — at the end. You wrote “a storm can’t ruin fun” in the middle and “it can’t ruin fun” again in the last sentence. When the lesson comes only at the end, it feels like a real ending. In the middle, tell what happened instead.

    Before: “It wasn’t bad, it was normal because a storm can’t ruin fun.”
    After: “However, when we got inside, we forgot about the storm and played just like on any other day.” (the lesson moves to the last sentence)

  4. 4. Use more exact words than normal. You used normal twice. It does not tell the reader much. Show what it was like instead: Did you forget about the storm? Did you play games? Exact words help the reader picture it. Also, came in does not say where you came in — got inside makes it clear.

    Before: “when we came in, it felt normal and fun”
    After: “when we got inside, we forgot about the storm and played just like on any other day”

  5. 5. Make the lesson sound like you thought about it. Your lesson is a good one! Telling it in your own fresh words makes it stronger than repeating the same phrase.

    Before: “After that, I learned that no matter what happens, it can’t ruin fun.”
    After: “That day, I learned that a bad start can’t ruin a fun day.”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Say “Caleb and I,” not “me and Caleb.” When you and a friend are the ones doing something, use I. A trick: take out the friend. You would say “I was going,” not “me was going.” Also, it is polite to name the other person first.

    Before: “me and Caleb were going to Main Event”
    After: “Caleb and I were going to Main Event”

  2. 2. Put a comma before and or but when it joins two full sentences. “a huge storm came” and “we were very disappointed” could each be a sentence by itself, so put a comma before and.

    Before: “a huge storm came and we were very disappointed”
    After: “a huge storm came, and we were very disappointed”

  3. 3. However starts a new sentence and has a comma after it. However is not like and or but — it cannot join two sentences with just a comma. End the first sentence with a period, then write However, with a capital H and a comma.

    Before: “We thought it was all over, however when we came in, it felt normal and fun.”
    After: “We thought the fun was all over. However, when we got inside, …”

  4. 4. Don’t join two sentences with only a comma. “It wasn’t bad” and “it was normal because a storm can’t ruin fun” are two full sentences. A comma alone is not strong enough to hold them together (this is called a comma splice). Use a period, or add and.

    Before: “It wasn’t bad, it was normal because a storm can’t ruin fun.”
    After: “It wasn’t bad. In fact, it was fun!” (In the polished story, this idea moved to the end — see #3 above.)

Did you use the three words?

  • however — Yes! “however when we came in” — next time start a new sentence with However, (see Grammar fix #3).
  • disappointed — Yes! “we were very disappointed”
  • relieved — Yes! “we were relieved that we still got to play”
