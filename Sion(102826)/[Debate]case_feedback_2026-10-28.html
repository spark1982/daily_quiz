Model answer

Thank you, judge. We negate. Notice what the resolution asks for: not better rules for data centers, but a nationwide freeze on building every new hyperscale campus. The question in this round is whether a blanket pause does more good than harm. We say it does not, for two reasons.

Contention one: a freeze kills the solutions to Pro’s own problem. Pro is worried about the power grid, but data center companies are now some of the biggest buyers of new clean energy in the country. Next, Harmon of the Midwest Energy Review, 2025 (practice card), finds that most new power contracts signed by hyperscale developers are for new wind, solar, and battery projects that would not be built without a large, steady customer. And, many states now require these campuses to pay for their own grid upgrades through large-load tariffs, so households are not left with the bill. A moratorium stops the buyers, so it stops the new generation too. The impact is a weaker grid, not a stronger one: demand for computing keeps growing from the centers that already exist, while the new clean power that would have served it never gets built. Further, the computing does not disappear. Hospitals, banks, and schools still need cloud services, so companies will build the same capacity in other countries, often on grids that burn more coal. That means more pollution worldwide and less American control over how this industry uses energy and water. So Contention one turns Pro’s case: if you care about clean, reliable power, you should want these buyers building here, under American rules.

Contention two: a freeze hurts the communities that chose to host these projects. Most hyperscale campuses go to rural counties with cheap land and existing power lines. Next, Delgado of the Rural Futures Institute, 2025 (practice card), reports that a single campus can become the largest property-tax payer in its county, funding schools, roads, and emergency services. And even when counties grant tax abatements, the equipment inside is replaced every few years and stays on the tax rolls, so revenue keeps coming long after construction ends. Further, each campus brings about fifteen hundred construction jobs and hundreds of permanent technician, security, and maintenance jobs. A federal freeze cancels projects these counties have already planned budgets around. That harm is immediate and certain: the day the moratorium passes, the paychecks and the tax money stop. And the harm spreads beyond the campus itself. Restaurants, hotels, and hardware stores near construction sites see their sales rise, and many electricians trained on these projects stay in the region afterward. Those are exactly the small-town jobs that are hardest to replace once they leave.

Now, on what Pro will say. They will tell you bills are rising. But bills rise for many reasons, including aging lines and fuel prices, and a pause on new buildings cannot lower bills caused by load that already exists. The real fix is local: make each new campus pay its own way, which states are already doing.

So weigh the round on probability and timeframe. Our harms happen the moment construction stops, in every county at once. Pro’s benefits depend on Congress writing new rules during the pause, which nothing in the resolution guarantees. When one side offers certain, immediate harm and the other offers a hope, the judge should negate.
