Reading Passage: The Amazon Rainforest

The Amazon rainforest, often called the "lungs of the Earth," spans more than 5.5 million square kilometers across nine countries in South America. It is home to an estimated 10 percent of all species on Earth, many of which have not yet been discovered or named by scientists. The forest plays a critical role in regulating the global climate by absorbing vast quantities of carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen through photosynthesis.


Despite its importance, the Amazon faces severe threats from deforestation. Agricultural expansion, illegal logging, and cattle ranching have led to the destruction of large swaths of forest. Between 2000 and 2020, the Amazon lost approximately 513,000 square kilometers of forest cover — an area larger than the country of Spain. Scientists warn that if deforestation continues at this rate, the Amazon could reach a "tipping point" beyond which it cannot recover its original function as a rainforest.


Indigenous communities have lived in the Amazon for thousands of years and have developed sophisticated knowledge of its ecosystems. These communities have traditionally served as stewards of the forest, using its resources sustainably without causing long-term damage. However, they have faced increasing pressure from outside forces, including displacement from their ancestral lands and exposure to diseases against which they have little natural immunity.


International efforts to protect the Amazon include financial incentives for countries to preserve forest cover and stricter regulations on deforestation. Some environmentalists argue that global cooperation is essential, since the Amazon's role in the climate system affects people far beyond its borders. Yet progress has been slow and uneven, with some governments prioritizing short-term economic gains over long-term environmental sustainability.
