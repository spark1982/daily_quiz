The topic

Topic

Describe a holiday your family celebrates and what you do.

Type of writing

Informative — Teach the reader about something, step by step.

How to write it

  1. Introduce your topic: name the holiday and tell when your family celebrates it.
  2. Tell what your family does to get ready (food, decorations, or plans).
  3. Explain what happens on the day, in order. Use First, Next, Then, and Finally.
  4. End with a closing sentence that tells why this holiday is special to your family.

Try to use these words

finally  ·  tradition  ·  delicious

What you did great

  • Your steps are in perfect order. “First, we set up the witches pot full of candy. Next, I go trick or treating… Then, we come back… Finally, when we’re done…” Each step starts with a time-order word and a comma. That is exactly how an informative paragraph teaches a reader.
  • You were honest about your family. “We don’t really have a special tradition… we just do it the normal way.” Telling the truth about what your family does makes your writing sound real, and you still used the word tradition.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your paragraph stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar is in its own section below.)

  1. 1. Add a closing sentence. The plan asked you to end with a sentence that tells why this holiday is special to your family. Your paragraph stops right after the last step, so the reader never hears why Halloween matters to you. I added one — next time, write your own real reason.

    Before: (no closing sentence)
    After: “Halloween is special to us because it is a fun night that we all spend together.”

  2. 2. Tell when the holiday is. Step 1 of the plan said to name the holiday and tell when your family celebrates it. Adding the date in your first sentence teaches the reader one more fact.

    Before: “One holiday we celebrate is Halloween.”
    After: “One holiday my family celebrates is Halloween on October 31.”

  3. 3. Give one or two more details in each step. An informative paragraph teaches, so small details help the reader picture it. Where do you put the pot? Where do you go trick-or-treating? Who gets your candy?

    Before: “First, we set up the witches pot full of candy.”
    After: “First, we fill our witch’s pot with candy and set it by the front door.”

    Before: “Next, I go trick or treating to get some candy.”
    After: “Next, I go trick-or-treating around our neighborhood to collect candy.”

  4. 4. Cut extra words that don’t add meaning. “A special tradition like doing something different for Halloween” says the same idea twice (special tradition already means doing something different). Shorter is clearer.

    Before: “We don’t really have a special tradition like doing something different for Halloween”
    After: “We don’t have a special tradition of our own.”

  5. 5. Choose an exact verb. “We just do it the normal way” uses do it, which is vague. Celebrate tells exactly what you do. In the last step, shares also shows your family eats the candy together.

    Before: “we just do it the normal way” / “my family gets to eat the delicious candy”
    After: “We just celebrate the normal way” / “my family shares the delicious candy we collected”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Don’t join two sentences with only a comma. “We don’t really have a special tradition…” and “we just do it the normal way…” are each complete sentences. A comma alone can’t hold two sentences together (that is called a comma splice). Use a period, or add a joining word like so or but.

    Before: “…doing something different for Halloween, we just do it the normal way…”
    After: “We don’t have a special tradition of our own. We just celebrate the normal way…”

  2. 2. Use an apostrophe to show who owns something. The pot belongs to a witch, so write witch’s (one witch) with an apostrophe + s. Witches with no apostrophe just means more than one witch.

    Before: “the witches pot”
    After: “our witch’s pot”

  3. 3. Write trick-or-treating with hyphens. When three words work together as one word for an activity, they are joined with hyphens (little dashes).

    Before: “trick or treating”
    After: “trick-or-treating”

  4. 4. Put a comma before and when it joins two sentences. “We come back” and “I get to give out some candy too” could each be a sentence, so a comma goes in front of and.

    Before: “Then, we come back and I get to give out some candy too.”
    After: “Then, we come back home, and I get to give out candy to other kids too.”
