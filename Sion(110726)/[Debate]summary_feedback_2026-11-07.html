  • You framed the burden correctly. “The Con is not claiming that hyperscale data centers are shiny and perfect. However, … the resolution only does more harm than good.” This keeps the round about the moratorium, not about whether data centers are good.

Model answer

Judge, this round comes down to one question: does a nationwide freeze actually fix the problems Pro describes? It doesn’t — and it costs rural communities real money. Here are the two things you should take from this speech.

First, extend our second contention: a pause won’t fix the grid, and that takes out Pro’s biggest argument. Pro’s case is about higher bills. But a pause can’t lower bills that come from data centers already running; that load stays on the grid either way. Meanwhile, new hyperscale contracts are what pay for new generation, much of it renewable. Freeze construction and you cancel those contracts, so the upgrades that would make power cheaper and more reliable get delayed. Pro’s only answer is that a pause “gives Congress time” to write national standards — but they never gave you evidence that Congress acts during the pause. So Pro’s impact depends on a chain of maybes, while our harm, delayed upgrades, starts the day the moratorium begins.

Second, extend our first contention: rural revenue and jobs. Hyperscale campuses bring billions in investment and property-tax revenue to counties that chose to host them. Pro says most jobs are temporary and tax breaks delay revenue. Two answers. One: even if a finished campus employs a few hundred people, in a small county that is a major employer, and it pays taxes every year for decades. Two: Pro’s tax-break point turns against them — if revenue already arrives late, a moratorium pushes it even later, or sends the project overseas so it never arrives at all.

And on water, remember the overbreadth: closed-loop and dry cooling already cut water use, and a nationwide pause also stops projects in water-rich regions. A siting rule solves Pro’s water harm without freezing everything, so water is not a reason to vote for this resolution.

So weigh the round this way. On probability, our impacts are certain — a freeze stops construction contracts and tax payments the day it passes — while Pro’s benefits require Congress to act and bills to fall from load that already exists. On timeframe, the grid upgrades and county revenue are lost now, and the pause only adds delay. And on reversibility, a canceled project rarely comes back; it moves to another country. Even if you buy that some bills rise, the moratorium doesn’t lower them — it only removes the investment that would. Please vote Con.
