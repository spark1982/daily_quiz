Topic

Explain how to get ready for school in the morning.

Type of writing

Informative — Teach the reader about something, step by step.

How to write it

  1. Start with a sentence that tells the reader what you will explain.
  2. Explain the steps in order. Use First, Next, Then, and Finally.
  3. Add a detail or a tip for at least two steps (why it matters or how to do it well).
  4. End with a closing sentence that tells why a good morning routine helps.

What you did great

  • Your steps are in a clear order. “First, you should wake up and get dressed. Next, you should brush your teeth.” A reader could follow your paragraph like a recipe — that is exactly what informative writing should do.
  • You told the reader why it matters. “If you do not do these, you might have trouble focusing in class” gives a real reason to follow the routine. Good writers don’t just list steps; they explain why.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your paragraph stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar is in its own section below.)

  1. 1. Start by telling the reader what you will explain. Your first sentence talks to the reader (“this will help you”), which is friendly, but it doesn’t say what the paragraph is about. A strong opening sentence names the topic so the reader knows what is coming.

    Before: “If you don’t know how to get ready for school, this will help you.”
    After: “Getting ready for school in the morning is easy if you follow a few simple steps.”

  2. 2. Give a tip or a reason for your steps. The plan asked for a detail on at least two steps. Your steps are short (“Next, you should brush your teeth.”), so the reader learns what to do but not why or how. Add a small “so” or “because” part. Here the shower sentence also joins the teeth step, because both are about getting clean.

    Before: “Next, you should brush your teeth. Taking a shower is optional, but you can if you want to.”
    After: “Next, brush your teeth, and take a quick shower if you have time, because it can help you feel refreshed.”

    Before: “First, you should wake up and get dressed.”
    After: “First, wake up and get dressed so you are ready to go.”

  3. 3. Use exact words instead of “stuff.” “Do some other refreshing stuff” doesn’t let the reader picture anything. What does getting organized look like? Name it! This is also a great place for a tip.

    Before: “Finally, you can get organized or do some other refreshing stuff and get to school.”
    After: “Finally, get organized by packing your backpack the night before, so you can grab it and leave for school on time.”

  4. 4. Give instructions directly. In how-to writing, you can tell the reader what to do: “Eat breakfast” instead of “you should eat breakfast.” It sounds clearer and more confident. Also tell what kind of breakfast and why.

    Before: “Then, you should eat breakfast and get energy stored up.”
    After: “Then, eat a healthy breakfast to give your body energy for the morning.”

  5. 5. Say the reason clearly and calmly. “You will regret it” sounds a little like a warning. The important idea is the focusing part, so keep that and say it plainly.

    Before: “It matters to do this, because if you do not do these, you might have trouble focusing in class and you will regret it.”
    After: “These steps matter because a rushed morning can make it hard to focus in class.”

  6. 6. Make your closing tell why a good routine helps. The plan’s last step was a closing sentence about why a morning routine helps. “Just in case if you don’t feel well” is a new idea that the paragraph never explained, so the reader is left wondering. End with the big benefit instead.

    Before: “This morning routine helps just in case if you don’t feel well.”
    After: “A good morning routine helps you start every school day calm, awake, and ready to learn.”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Use “just in case” OR “if” — not both. “Just in case” already means “if this happens,” so adding if says the same thing twice.

    Before: “just in case if you don’t feel well”
    After: “just in case you don’t feel well”

  2. 2. Tell the reader what “these” are. “If you do not do these” leaves the reader guessing. Put a noun after this, these, or those.

    Before: “It matters to do this, because if you do not do these”
    After: “These steps matter because if you skip them”

  3. 3. Put the words in the usual order. “Get energy stored up” is hard to read. In English we say “store up energy” (action first, then the thing) — or just “get energy.”

    Before: “eat breakfast and get energy stored up”
    After: “eat breakfast to store up energy”

  4. 4. Finish the sentence after “can.” “You can if you want to” is okay when talking, but in writing it is clearer to say what you can do.

    Before: “Taking a shower is optional, but you can if you want to.”
    After: “Taking a shower is optional, but you can take one if you want to.”

Did you use the three words?

  • Finally — Yes! “Finally, you can get organized” — a perfect last step.
  • organized — Yes! “get organized” — next time add how (see fix #3 above).
  • refreshed — Almost! You wrote “refreshing,” which is a cousin of the word. Try the exact word next time: “a shower helps you feel refreshed.”
