What you did great

  • You noticed the most important thing in the picture. “The wind is pulling the man’s umbrella and he was grabbing onto it so it was grabbing him too” is a smart observation — you saw that holding on tight is exactly why the man is being pulled.
  • You thought about feelings and what comes next. “The man probably felt pretty scared, because he might have never experienced this before” gives a feeling and a reason for it. Then you made a prediction about the sea. That is just what the plan asked for.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your paragraph stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar is in its own section below.)

  1. 1. Look carefully at where the picture happens. You wrote that the man is “by a lake,” but later you wrote “the sea.” The photo shows a seashore with big ocean waves, so it is the sea. A reader gets confused when the place changes in the middle. Check the picture and use one place the whole time.

    Before: “He is by a lake, and he was getting pulled.”
    After: “A man was taking a walk by the sea when, suddenly, a strong gust of wind hit him.” (Your first two sentences were joined, so the reader learns who, where, and what happened right away.)

  2. 2. Use exact describing words for the weather. “The weather is very strong” is hard to picture — weather can be stormy, windy, or rough, but the wind is what is strong. And “the sea might be getting pulled” doesn’t show what the water looks like. Tell what you can see: waves.

    Before: “The weather is very strong, and the sea might be getting pulled by the wind as well.”
    After: “The weather was stormy, and the wind was whipping the sea into rough, foamy waves.”

  3. 3. Split a long sentence so each action is clear. Your umbrella sentence holds two ideas: the wind pulls the umbrella, and the umbrella pulls the man. Two shorter sentences let the reader see each step. A strong action verb like tug or drag also helps the reader feel the pull.

    Before: “The wind is pulling the man’s umbrella and he was grabbing onto it so it was grabbing him too.”
    After: “The wind tugged hard on his umbrella and tried to rip it out of his hands. He held on tight, so the umbrella dragged him along the shore.”

  4. 4. Make your prediction easy to follow. Your sentence about what happens next is a great idea, but it twists around: “is probably not pulling … and pull him … and make him drenched.” Say the prediction in a simple way first, then give your reason in the next sentence.

    Before: “The wind is probably not pulling in different directions and pull him into the sea and make him drenched in water from getting pulled.”
    After: “Luckily, the wind will probably not drag him into the sea and leave him drenched.”

  5. 5. Finish with a clear reason sentence. “I think that because …” leaves the reader asking, “Think what?” Tell the reason plainly. Your idea that wind changes direction is a good one!

    Before: “I think that because wind can pull in different directions, not only in one direction every time.”
    After: “I think this because the wind often changes direction instead of blowing the same way every time.”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Stay in one tense (past) for the whole story. You started in the past (“was going,” “ran into”) but then jumped to the present (“He is by a lake,” “The wind is pulling”) and back again. When you tell what happened in a picture like a story, keep using the past tense.

    Before: “He is by a lake … The wind is pulling the man’s umbrella and he was grabbing onto it”
    After: “He was by the sea … The wind tugged hard on his umbrella, and he held on tight”

  2. 2. “A gust of wind,” not “a gust of strong winds.” A gust is one sudden rush of wind, so it goes with wind (one), not winds. Put the describing word in front of gust.

    Before: “a gust of strong winds”
    After: “a strong gust of wind”

  3. 3. Put a comma before and or so when they join two sentences. “The wind is pulling the man’s umbrella” and “he was grabbing onto it” could each be a sentence, so put a comma before and.

    Before: “The wind is pulling the man’s umbrella and he was grabbing onto it so it was grabbing him too.”
    After: “The wind was pulling the man’s umbrella, and he was holding onto it, so it pulled him too.”

  4. 4. No comma before because here. When because comes in the middle and gives the reason, you usually don’t need a comma.

    Before: “The man probably felt pretty scared, because he might have never experienced this before.”
    After: “The man probably felt scared because he might never have been in a storm this strong before.”

  5. 5. Put never right after the first helping verb. With two helping verbs (might have), never goes between them: might never have.

    Before: “he might have never experienced this before”
    After: “he might never have experienced this before”

  6. 6. Keep verbs that go together in the same form. In “is probably not pulling … and pull him … and make him,” the verbs don’t match (pulling, pull, make). After will, use the plain form for every verb: will not drag … and leave.

    Before: “is probably not pulling in different directions and pull him into the sea and make him drenched”
    After: “will probably not drag him into the sea and leave him drenched”

Did you use the three words?

  • Suddenly — Yes! “until suddenly he ran into a gust” — it was a great way to show the surprise.
  • gust — Yes! “a gust of strong winds” — next time say “a strong gust of wind” (see Grammar fix #2).
  • drenched — Yes! “make him drenched in water” — drenched already means “very wet,” so you can just say “leave him drenched.”

Your writing, polished

A man was taking a walk by the sea when, suddenly, a strong gust of wind hit him. The wind tugged hard on his umbrella and tried to rip it out of his hands. He held on tight, so the umbrella dragged him along the shore. The weather was stormy, and the wind was whipping the sea into rough, foamy waves. The man probably felt scared because he might never have been in a storm this strong before. Luckily, the wind will probably not drag him into the sea and leave him drenched. I think this because the wind often changes direction instead of blowing the same way every time.
