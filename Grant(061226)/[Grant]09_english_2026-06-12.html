Passage 1 — The Science of Volcanoes

Deep beneath the Earth's surface, pockets of molten rock called magma collect in chambers under intense pressure. When the pressure becomes too great for the surrounding rock to contain, the magma forces its way upward through cracks and vents. As it reaches the surface, it erupts as lava, ash, and gases. This process is what creates a volcano. Over time, repeated eruptions build up layers of hardened lava and ash that form the distinctive cone-shaped mountains we recognize today.

Not all volcanoes look the same or behave the same way. Shield volcanoes, like those found in Hawaii, have gently sloping sides built from thin, fast-moving lava flows that spread over wide areas. Stratovolcanoes, also called composite volcanoes, are the towering, steep-sided peaks such as Mount Fuji in Japan and Mount St. Helens in Washington. These are built from alternating layers of ash and thick lava and are associated with more explosive eruptions. A third type, cinder cone volcanoes, are smaller and form from fragments of solidified lava that fall around the vent opening.

Despite the destruction they can cause, volcanoes have played an important role in shaping life on Earth. Volcanic eruptions release gases that are believed to have contributed to Earth's early atmosphere. The ash and minerals deposited by eruptions break down over time into exceptionally fertile soil. Regions near volcanoes, such as parts of Indonesia and Central America, are known for their highly productive farmland. Many communities have thrived for centuries in the shadow of active volcanoes precisely because the land is so rich.

Scientists who study volcanoes are called volcanologists. They monitor volcanic activity using a wide range of instruments, including seismographs that detect underground tremors, GPS sensors that measure the swelling of the ground as magma rises, and gas detectors that track changes in sulfur dioxide emissions. By analyzing these signals together, volcanologists can often predict when an eruption is likely and help communities prepare or evacuate in time.

Passage 2 — Earthquakes and Tectonic Forces

The Earth's outer shell is divided into large pieces called tectonic plates, which move slowly — just a few centimeters each year. When two plates slide past each other, collide, or pull apart, enormous amounts of energy are released. This energy travels through the ground as seismic waves, which we feel as an earthquake. The point underground where the shaking originates is called the focus, and the point on the surface directly above it is called the epicenter.

Scientists called seismologists use instruments called seismographs to record and study earthquakes. A seismograph detects even tiny vibrations in the ground and draws them as a series of waves on a readout. By comparing the arrival times of different types of seismic waves at multiple stations, seismologists can calculate exactly where an earthquake originated. The magnitude of an earthquake — how much energy it releases — is measured on the Richter scale or the newer Moment Magnitude Scale.
