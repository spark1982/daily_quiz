The topic

Topic

Explain how to make your favorite snack, step by step.

Type of writing

Informative — Teach the reader about something, step by step.

How to write it

  1. Introduce your topic: name your favorite snack and say why it is worth making.
  2. Tell what you need (the ingredients and tools).
  3. Explain the steps in order. Use First, Next, Then, and Finally.
  4. End with a closing sentence, like how it tastes or a tip for the reader.

Sentence starter

“My favorite snack is easy to make, and you only need a few things.” (You can use this or write your own first sentence.)

What you did great

  • Perfect step order. “First,” “Next,” “Then,” and “Finally,” walk the reader through the recipe one step at a time — that is exactly how an informative paragraph should be organized.
  • Words you can almost taste. “Fry the triangles you made in hot oil until they are crispy and golden” tells the reader exactly what to look for, so they know when the chips are done.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your paragraph stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar is in its own section below.)

  1. 1. Tell the reader what they need before the steps. The plan asked you to list the ingredients and tools. A reader can’t start cooking if they don’t know they need a rolling pin, a knife, and a pot of oil. Put everything in one sentence right after your first sentence.

    Before: (no list of what you need)
    After: “All you need is corn flour, water, salt, a rolling pin, a knife, a pot of oil, and an adult to help.”

  2. 2. Add a safety step for the hot oil. Frying in hot oil can burn you. A good how-to writer always warns the reader about the dangerous part. Two words fix it: with an adult’s help.

    Before: “Then, fry the triangles you made in hot oil until they are crispy and golden.”
    After: “Then, with an adult’s help, fry the triangles in hot oil until they are crispy and golden.”

  3. 3. Say why your snack is worth making. Your first sentence says it is easy — great! The plan also asked why it is worth making. One short reason makes the reader want to try it.

    Before: “Making Doritos is easier than you think if you follow these simple steps.”
    After: “Making my own Doritos is easier than you think, and they taste even better fresh and warm.”

  4. 4. Give tiny details that help the recipe work. Exact details turn a good recipe into one that really works. Thin dough fries crispy, and cheese powder sticks best while the chips are still warm.

    Before: “roll the dough into circles” / “carefully sprinkle them with cheese powder”
    After: “roll the dough into thin, flat circles” / “carefully sprinkle them with cheese powder while they are still warm”

  5. 5. Talk straight to the reader. Your third and fourth steps already sound like a recipe: “Then, fry the triangles…” Do the same for the first two steps instead of repeating you have to. Recipes are written as commands.

    Before: “First, you have to get corn flour, water, and salt, so you can mix them together to make dough.”
    After: “First, mix the corn flour, water, and salt together to make a dough.”

  6. 6. Use the simplest word. Triangle shapes says the same thing twice — a triangle is already a shape. And the triangles you made can just be the triangles.

    Before: “cut them into triangle shapes”
    After: “cut them into triangles”

  7. 7. Make your last tip sound like a tip. Ending with a way to eat the chips is a great idea! Name the sauce so the reader can picture it.

    Before: “You can eat them plain, or you can put them in sauce and eat it.”
    After: “You can eat them plain or dip them in salsa for an extra kick.”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. Use only one joining word: so or then, not both. “So then” sounds like talking. In writing, pick one.

    Before: “roll the dough into circles so then you can cut them”
    After: “roll the dough into thin, flat circles so you can cut them”

  2. 2. Make the pronoun match. You wrote them (the chips) and then it for the same chips. Many chips = them every time.

    Before: “you can put them in sauce and eat it.”
    After: “dip them in salsa”

  3. 3. No comma before so when it means “so that.” In “get corn flour, water, and salt, so you can mix them,” the word so tells the reason (so that you can mix them). That kind of so does not need a comma in front.

    Before: “water, and salt, so you can mix them together”
    After: “water, and salt so you can mix them together” (or just: “mix the corn flour, water, and salt together”)

Every word is spelled correctly, and your list “corn flour, water, and salt” has its commas in exactly the right places — nice!
