The 1AC you are answering

Because too many Americans cannot get the care they need, my partner and I affirm: Resolved: The United States federal government should establish national health insurance in the United States.

Our plan: The United States federal government will establish a single, publicly financed national health insurance program that covers every person residing in the United States for hospital care, doctor visits, prescription drugs, and mental health care, with no premiums, deductibles, or copays at the point of service. The program will be funded through a dedicated payroll tax and a progressive income surcharge, and it will negotiate prices for drugs and services nationwide. Implementation begins one year after passage.

Observation one: Inherency. The current system leaves millions behind by design. Coverage depends on a job, an income cutoff, or the state you live in. Harlan, Journal of Coverage Policy, 2025 (practice card): “Even after a decade of reforms, roughly 26 million residents still have no insurance at all, and the patchwork of employer plans, marketplace subsidies, and state Medicaid rules means coverage can vanish the moment a worker changes jobs or a family crosses an income line. No current federal proposal closes this gap.” And, the gap is not closing on its own. Harlan continues: “Temporary subsidy boosts have expired or face expiration, and several states have declined to expand Medicaid, so the uninsured count has stopped falling.” Next, job-based coverage is eroding. Morrow, Labor and Benefits Monitor, 2025 (practice card): “Employers are shifting more costs onto workers every year. Average family premiums have risen far faster than wages over the past two decades, and a growing share of small businesses no longer offer health benefits at all. For millions of workers, the coverage they have is one layoff away from disappearing.”

Advantage one: Lives. First, being uninsured kills. Okonkwo, American Review of Public Health, 2024 (practice card): “Uninsured adults are significantly less likely to receive preventive screenings and more likely to be diagnosed with cancer at a late stage. Our cohort analysis associates lack of coverage with a 25 percent higher risk of death among working-age adults, which translates to tens of thousands of preventable deaths each year.” Next, underinsurance is nearly as dangerous. Delgado, Health Equity Quarterly, 2025 (practice card): “About four in ten insured adults report skipping or delaying care because of deductibles and copays. When cost sharing rises, patients cut back on necessary medications for blood pressure and diabetes, not just unnecessary visits.” So the impact is clear: every year the status quo continues, thousands of Americans die from conditions that are treatable when they are caught early.

Advantage two: Financial security. First, medical bills push families into debt. Whitfield, Center for Household Finance, 2025 (practice card): “Medical debt is the most common type of debt in collections in the United States, and illness or medical bills contribute to a majority of personal bankruptcies we studied.” And, the system wastes money that could fund care. Brennan, Fiscal Health Institute, 2024 (practice card): “Administrative costs consume a far larger share of U.S. health spending than in single-payer countries. A unified public program could save between 300 and 500 billion dollars a year in billing, marketing, and claims processing, more than enough to cover the uninsured.” Next, debt itself makes people sicker. Whitfield, 2025 (practice card): “Households carrying medical debt are more likely to postpone future care, skip prescriptions, and report anxiety and depression, creating a cycle in which one illness leads to the next. Ending surprise bills breaks that cycle.” The impact: families stop choosing between rent and a hospital bill, and the nation spends less overall.

Observation two: Solvency. First, universal coverage works. Ishikawa, Comparative Health Systems Review, 2023 (practice card): “Every other wealthy democracy guarantees coverage to all residents, and these nations achieve longer life expectancy and lower infant mortality while spending less per person than the United States.” Next, removing cost at the point of service changes behavior. Delgado again, 2025 (practice card): “When financial barriers are removed, patients schedule checkups earlier and fill prescriptions they previously skipped.” And, national price negotiation controls costs. Brennan, 2024 (practice card): “Single-buyer bargaining is the main reason drug prices abroad are a fraction of U.S. prices.” Further, the plan reaches the people the current system misses. Harlan, 2025 (practice card): “Because enrollment in a universal program is automatic at birth or residency, no one falls through the cracks because of paperwork, missed deadlines, or a change in income. Programs that enroll people automatically reach nearly everyone they are meant to cover, while programs that require people to sign up leave millions eligible but uninsured.”

Now, on what the Negative will say: they will claim the plan costs too much. But Brennan shows administrative savings alone offset much of the price, and families already pay through premiums and deductibles; the plan simply replaces those costs with a fairer tax. They may also say the status quo can be fixed piece by piece. But Harlan proves that a decade of piece-by-piece reform still left 26 million people uninsured, and every patch can be undone by the next Congress. So weigh the round this way: on magnitude, our advantages reach tens of millions of people; on probability, the link between coverage and survival is backed by cohort data; and on timeframe, people are dying and going bankrupt right now. Any risk of saving those lives outweighs the Negative’s speculative cost arguments. We are proud to affirm.

  • You respected the Negative’s job. “The Negative concedes that these are genuine problems… However, the Negative is going to explain why the Affirmative plan will not solve these issues.” Conceding harms on purpose and attacking solvency is a real Policy strategy, and you explained it clearly to the judge.

What to improve

  • Don’t drop Inherency. The 1AC’s own Harlan card only says the uninsured count “has stopped falling.” One short paragraph would do it: “Their own card shows the status quo is a policy choice, not a barrier — marketplace subsidies, auto-enrollment, and Medicaid expansion can solve most of the harm without a whole new system.” Even 60 seconds here forces the 2AC to spend time defending it.
  • Match your roadmap to your speech. You said, “I will be addressing the Affirmative’s Solvency… so only solvency needs to be flowed,” but your cost argument answers Advantage two. Try: “Two sheets: Solvency, then Advantage two.” Judges flow by sheets, and a wrong roadmap can make them put your arguments in the wrong place.
  • Bridge your Canada evidence to this plan. The 2AC will say, “Canada’s waits come from its own budget limits; other single-payer systems don’t have them.” Pre-empt that by tying the evidence to the plan text: “Canada’s waits happen for the same reason this plan creates them — everyone is covered, care is free at the point of service, and supply does not grow.” Also expect the 2AC to attack the source: an opinion column from a policy think tank is weaker than a peer-reviewed study, so in a real round, pair it with a second, more neutral card.
  • Give the cost argument a warrant and evidence. “There is a high likelihood that… costs skyrocket” is a claim without proof. Name the mechanism: the plan’s savings depend on paying hospitals less; if payments are cut, hospitals close or ration care; if they are not cut, the new taxes must rise. Then read a card for it.
  • Answer their weighing, not just their case. The 1AC said “any risk of saving those lives outweighs.” Flip it: “Our turn comes straight from their plan text and their own Delgado card, so it is more probable; it starts the year the plan starts; and it affects every patient, not just the uninsured.”
  • Small style fix: keep notes to the judge (“Let it be known that if this were a real round…”) out of the speech itself. You are right that a real 1NC usually reads off-case positions like a disadvantage or counterplan; this drill only asks for on-case attacks, so just start with your roadmap.

Where the 1AC was weakest

The 1AC was written with these hidden weaknesses. You found the second one; the first and third were still open.

  • Inherency: the 1AC ignores status-quo mechanisms that are expanding coverage (ACA marketplaces, continuing state Medicaid expansions, auto-enrollment) — Harlan only says the decline has 'stopped falling', not that it cannot be solved in the status quo; a Neg can argue incremental reforms solve most of the harm.
  • Solvency deficit: coverage ≠ access. The plan adds tens of millions of patients with no increase in doctors, nurses, or hospital capacity; removing cost sharing raises demand, so wait times and provider shortages could block the very screenings Advantage one depends on. Ishikawa is a correlation between countries (longer life expectancy has many causes — diet, violence, poverty), not proof that the plan causes the impact.
  • Impact / cost problems: Okonkwo is an 'associates' cohort finding (correlation, possible confounders like income); the 25% figure is turned into 'tens of thousands of deaths' without warrant. Brennan's $300–500B savings is one estimate that ignores transition costs, provider payment cuts, and the size of the new payroll/income taxes; 'any risk of saving lives' framing skips probability.

What the 1AC did well (you have to respect this): its harms evidence is broad (uninsured, underinsured, and medical debt), and its pre-emption already answered “it costs too much” with the Brennan savings card. That is why a pure cost attack without new evidence is risky — the capacity turn was the better place to spend your time.

Names for these patterns: treating “associated with” as proof of cause is a correlation-vs-causation problem (Okonkwo, Ishikawa), and assuming coverage automatically means care is a solvency deficit.

Off-time roadmap: two sheets — inherency, then solvency, which I will turn on both advantages.

My partner and I negate. Before I start, notice what the Negative will not do: we will not pretend that people without insurance or with medical debt are fine. Those are real problems. Our case is that the status quo is already fixing them faster than the Affirmative admits, and that the plan as written would make care harder, not easier, to get.

First, Observation one: Inherency. Look at the Affirmative’s own Harlan card. It says the uninsured count “has stopped falling.” It does not say it cannot fall. That card describes a policy choice, not a barrier. Lowe, Health Access Review, 2026 (practice card): “Automatic re-enrollment, permanent marketplace subsidies, and new state Medicaid expansions together could cut the uninsured rate by more than a third within five years without replacing anyone’s current coverage.” So, extend this: if targeted fixes can reach most of the 26 million, the harms are being solved in the status quo, and the Affirmative has to prove why their whole-system replacement is needed instead.

Second, on Solvency — their plan has no capacity. Read the plan text again: it adds every resident and removes all premiums, deductibles, and copays, but it adds no doctors, no nurses, and no hospital beds. Their own Delgado card says that when cost barriers disappear, patients “schedule checkups earlier and fill prescriptions.” Good — but that is a demand increase. Okafor, Workforce Policy Quarterly, 2025 (practice card): “The country already faces a shortage of primary-care physicians, and a sudden rise in visits without new providers would push average wait times for a new-patient appointment from weeks to months.” Coverage is not the same as care. A card that says you are insured does not get you a screening if there is no appointment.

And this turns Advantage one. Their Okonkwo card says the danger is late diagnosis. Long waits for screenings cause exactly that. So the plan does not just fail to save lives — it risks delaying the very screenings their advantage depends on. Also notice that Okonkwo only says lack of coverage is “associated” with higher death risk. Uninsured adults are also more likely to be low-income, and income affects health in many ways. Correlation is not proof that a coverage card alone saves those lives.

Next, Ishikawa is a correlation too. Other wealthy countries do live longer, but diet, gun violence, car accidents, and poverty all differ. The card never isolates the insurance system as the cause, so it does not prove this plan solves.

Now, Advantage two: Financial security. Brennan’s 300 to 500 billion dollars is one estimate of administrative savings. It does not count transition costs or the new payroll tax and income surcharge in their own plan. Ward, Public Finance Monitor, 2025 (practice card): “Single-payer cost estimates depend almost entirely on cutting payments to hospitals; if those cuts are smaller than planned, the new taxes must rise, and if they are larger, rural hospitals may close.” Either way, the plan trades medical bills for higher taxes or fewer hospitals. That is not financial security.

Also on Solvency, their automatic-enrollment card. Harlan says automatic programs “reach nearly everyone they are meant to cover.” Fine — but that proves automatic enrollment works, not that a single national plan is needed to get it. The status quo can auto-enroll people into Medicaid and marketplace plans today. So this card actually helps the Negative on inherency: the tool that solves coverage gaps already fits inside the current system.

Further, notice what their harms really need. The 1AC’s deaths impact depends on early screenings, and its debt impact depends on lower bills. Both only happen if patients can actually see a provider and if the new taxes cost less than the old premiums. The 1AC never proves either one. That means every one of their impacts runs through solvency, and solvency is exactly where the plan breaks.

So weigh it this way. On probability, our capacity argument comes straight from their plan text and their own Delgado card — more demand, no new supply. On timeframe, waits begin the year the plan starts, while their benefits depend on care people cannot reach. On magnitude, the turn affects every patient in the country, not just the uninsured. Any risk of the turn outweighs, because it means the plan can make the exact harm they describe worse. Vote Negative. I am ready for cross-examination.
