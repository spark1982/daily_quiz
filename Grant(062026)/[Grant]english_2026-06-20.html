Inside a single beehive, thousands of worker bees depend on one another, and finding food quickly is one of their most urgent tasks. When a scout bee discovers a rich patch of flowers, it cannot simply tell the others in words. Instead, honeybees use one of the most precise forms of communication in the animal kingdom: a movement pattern called the waggle dance. Unlike ants, which rely on scent trails along the ground, bees use a visual performance to share spatial information, and scientists have studied this behavior for decades.

The waggle dance follows a strict pattern. A scout bee returning from a flower patch moves in a tight figure-eight on the honeycomb's vertical surface, shaking its body during the straight middle portion of each loop. The angle of this straight run, measured against an imaginary line toward the sun, tells the other bees the exact direction of the food. The length of time spent shaking, called the waggle phase, signals distance: a longer waggle phase means the flowers are farther away. Bees behind the dancer sense these signals through touch in the dark hive, almost as if the dancer were tracing a tiny map in the air.

Once a scout finishes several rounds of the dance, a group of attentive follower bees leaves the hive and flies toward the indicated food. If the source is as rich as the scout suggested, these bees return and perform their own waggle dances, recruiting still more workers. Within a short time, dozens of bees may travel back and forth to the same patch of flowers, all guided by a dance that began with one bee's discovery.

In recent years, honeybee colonies worldwide have faced serious decline, a problem researchers call colony collapse disorder. Pesticide exposure can interfere with a bee's navigation and disrupt the waggle dance's accuracy, while the loss of wildflower meadows has reduced the food scouts can find. Parasites and disease add further strain on weakened colonies. Beekeepers and researchers now plant pollinator-friendly gardens and limit harmful pesticides, hoping to protect a species responsible for pollinating roughly one third of the food crops people eat.
