Bioluminescence: Living Light in the Deep Sea

Far below the ocean's surface, in a region where no sunlight ever reaches, thousands of creatures make their own light. This ability is called bioluminescence, and it is far more common in the deep sea than most people realize. Scientists estimate that the majority of animals living below two hundred meters can produce light in some form, from tiny plankton to fish nearly as long as a car.

Bioluminescence works through a chemical reaction between a molecule called luciferin and an enzyme called luciferase. When the two combine in the presence of oxygen, they release energy as light rather than heat. Because almost none of the energy is wasted as heat, scientists often call this glow cold light, in contrast to a lightbulb, which wastes a great deal of energy warming the air around it.

Deep sea animals use their light for very different reasons. Anglerfish dangle a glowing lure above their mouths to attract curious prey close enough to swallow. Some squid squirt clouds of glowing ink to confuse predators before darting away. Other fish use a trick called counter-illumination: they produce a faint glow on their undersides that matches the dim light filtering down from above, erasing the dark silhouette that would otherwise give them away to hunters swimming below.

Most bioluminescent light appears blue or green, since these colors travel farthest through seawater. A handful of fish, however, have evolved the rare ability to produce and see red light, a wavelength invisible to nearly every other deep sea creature. This lets them illuminate prey like a hidden spotlight that only they can perceive.

Sailors have reported glowing milky seas for centuries, long before scientists understood the cause. Today, researchers study a molecule called green fluorescent protein, first isolated from a glowing jellyfish, to track individual cells inside living organisms. Despite decades of ocean exploration, scientists believe that countless bioluminescent species living in the deep sea have still never been observed by human eyes.
