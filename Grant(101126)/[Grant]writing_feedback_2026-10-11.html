The topic

Topic

You are the first kid to visit the Moon.

Type of writing

Narrative — Tell a story with a beginning, middle, and end.

How to write it

  1. Beginning: Tell who you are, and how you get to the Moon.
  2. Middle: Tell one or two exciting things that happen on the Moon, in order.
  3. Add details: What do you see, hear, and feel? What is it like to walk there?
  4. End: Tell how you get home and how you feel about your trip.

Sentence starter

“The rocket shook as the countdown reached zero, and I squeezed my seat with both hands.” (You can use this or write your own first sentence.)

Try to use these words

suddenly  ·  dusty  ·  bouncy

Task: Write one paragraph (60–120 words). Use the plan above and try to use the three words.

What you did great

  • You answered every sense question in the plan. “I felt super light when I walked,” “I saw nothing but grey and black,” and “I didn’t really hear anything” tell what you felt, saw, and heard. They are also true science: the Moon has weak gravity, gray rocks, and no air to carry sound.
  • You picked strong action words. “I squeezed my seat” and “I jolted in my seat” let the reader feel the rocket shaking, and “I was able to do a backflip!” is an exciting moment that makes the reader smile.

Let’s make it even better

Here is everything that will make your story stronger, starting with the most important. (Grammar mistakes are in their own section below.)

  1. 1. Tell the end of the trip: how you get home. The last step of the plan says to tell how you get home. Your story jumps from the Moon straight to going to sleep, so the reader wonders, “Wait, how did he get back to Earth?” Add one sentence about the trip home before you go to sleep.

    Before: “I went to sleep feeling amazing…”
    After: “Finally, I climbed back into the rocket and zoomed home to Earth. That night, I went to sleep feeling amazing…”

  2. 2. Keep the story in time order. Your first sentence shows the rocket already shaking at zero. Then the second sentence jumps ahead (“I got to the moon”), and the third sentence goes back to the rocket lifting off. A story is easiest to follow when each sentence happens after the one before it. Introduce yourself first, then tell the launch.

    Before: “The rocket shook as the countdown reached zero… My name is Grant, and I got to the moon with a rocket ship. It started lifting suddenly…”
    After: “My name is Grant, and I am the first kid to visit the Moon. The rocket shook as the countdown reached zero… Suddenly, it started lifting, and I jolted in my seat.”

  3. 3. Say who or what is bouncy. “It was very bouncy” sounds like the Moon is a trampoline. Really, you were bouncy, because the Moon pulls on you much less than Earth does. Make the sentence say that.

    Before: “When I got to the moon, it was very bouncy, and I was able to do a backflip!”
    After: “When I got to the Moon, every step was bouncy, so I was able to do a backflip!”

  4. 4. Show what the crater was like. “Seeing what it was like” makes the reader ask, “Well, what was it like?” Tell them! This is also a perfect spot for your word dusty.

    Before: “I was also able to jump in the craters seeing what it was like. It was pretty dusty, but, I didn’t care.”
    After: “I also jumped into a crater, and my boots sank into soft, dusty ground, but I didn’t care.”

  5. 5. Show your feeling instead of saying “I knew it was fun.” “I knew it was fun” tells the reader a fact. Showing how you acted lets them feel the fun with you.

    Before: “I didn’t really hear anything, but I knew it was fun.”
    After: “It was totally silent, but I was grinning the whole time.”

  6. 6. Make room by cutting repeats. Your paragraph is 119 words, right at the top of the 60–120 range, so adding the trip home needs some space. “I was able to” appears two times. Saying it more simply saves words.

    Before: “I was also able to jump in the craters”
    After: “I also jumped into a crater”

Grammar fixes

  1. 1. “Moon” gets a capital letter. When you mean our Moon, the one in the sky, it is a name, so it starts with a capital M (just like in the topic). You wrote “moon” three times.

    Before: “I got to the moon”
    After: “I got to the Moon”

  2. 2. No comma right after “but” here. Put one comma before “but” when it joins two sentences, and none after it.

    Before: “It was pretty dusty, but, I didn’t care.”
    After: “It was pretty dusty, but I didn’t care.”

  3. 3. You travel in a rocket ship. We ride in cars, planes, and rockets. “With a rocket ship” sounds like the rocket came along beside you.

    Before: “I got to the moon with a rocket ship.”
    After: “I got to the Moon in a rocket ship.”

  4. 4. Use “to” to tell why you did something, and “into” for moving inside. You jumped into the craters to see what they were like. “Seeing” doesn’t show the reason.

    Before: “jump in the craters seeing what it was like”
    After: “jump into the craters to see what they were like”

  5. 5. Keep it simple: “felt,” not “had felt.” The whole story is already in the past, so plain past tense works best here.

    Before: “about what the moon had felt like”
    After: “about what the Moon felt like”

  6. 6. In America, we usually spell it “gray.” “Grey” isn’t wrong (it is the British spelling), but American books and tests use “gray.”

    Before: “nothing but grey and black”
    After: “nothing but gray and black”

Every sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with the right mark, and your exclamation point after the backflip fits perfectly. Nice work!
