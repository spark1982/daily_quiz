Your opponent's constructive

We negate the resolution: Resolved: The United States federal government should enact a moratorium on hyperscale data center construction. Hyperscale data centers are the giant facilities, often larger than ten football fields, that run cloud storage, streaming, and artificial intelligence for millions of users. A moratorium is a nationwide pause, so the question in this round is not whether data centers have costs, but whether stopping all new construction is better than managing those costs.

Contention one is local economies. Hyperscale campuses are usually built in rural counties because land is cheap and power lines are nearby. Once they open, they pay property taxes on billions of dollars of equipment. Harmon of the Midwest Energy Review, 2025, a practice card, finds that a single hyperscale campus can pay more in local property taxes than every other business in a rural county combined. That money funds schools, roads, and emergency services. Construction also matters: one campus employs about fifteen hundred building-trade workers over two years. A federal moratorium freezes projects that counties have already planned their budgets around, so the harm is immediate.

Data centers also support the businesses around them. Delgado of the Rural Futures Institute, 2025, a practice card, reports that towns near new campuses saw restaurant, hotel, and hardware-store sales rise during construction, and that many electricians trained on these projects stayed to work in the region afterward. When the federal government freezes construction, it is not pausing one building; it is pausing the paychecks and small-business income that come with it.

Contention two is the climate. Demand for computing does not disappear when the United States stops building. Cloud services and artificial intelligence keep growing, so companies will expand in other countries instead, often in places that run their grids on coal. Okafor of Global Grid Watch, 2025, a practice card, reports that about sixty percent of new power contracts signed by American data centers in 2024 were for wind or solar energy. When those projects move overseas, those clean-energy contracts move away too, and American rules on emissions and water recycling no longer apply. The impact is higher global emissions and less American control over how this industry grows.

There is also a security cost. Hospitals, banks, and government agencies rely on cloud computing every day. When new capacity is built abroad instead of at home, more sensitive American data is stored and processed under the laws of other countries. Keeping construction in the United States lets American regulators set the rules for privacy, cybersecurity, and clean energy at the same time.

Our opponents will tell you that data centers raise electricity bills and use too much water. Those are real concerns, but they are local problems with local solutions. States can require companies to pay for their own grid upgrades and to cool their servers with recycled water. Several states are already writing those rules, and companies have every reason to follow them because they want permission to keep building. A nationwide freeze punishes every community, even the ones where data centers are working well.

So weigh the round this way. Our impacts are more probable and come sooner: the moment construction stops, counties lose revenue and workers lose jobs, while the harms on the other side depend on local conditions that regulation can already fix. For these reasons, we are proud to negate.

The affirmative will start off by addressing the first contention: local economies. The negative claims that hyperscale data centers actually benefit the local economies of the rural counties they are often built in. However, the benefits they describe are highly exaggerated. Mineo 26, a card from The Harvard Gazette, highlights how the increased employment promised by the Negative is "a significant false promise". The card highlights how developers say that hyper scale data centers bring jobs because it is attractive to policy makers. In practice, the construction requires brief work for only a year or two to get the center built. After construction, it requires very few people to run, and even during construction labor can be local and unionized, with trade professionals coming in from other states. This card also shows how the increased tax revenue and other supposed community benefits unfortunately have "very low economic development that plays out on the local level." There is a small amount of tax revenue, but tax break policies -- policies made by the government that reduce how much tax an individual or business has to pay -- reduce that already small amount. Last year, Virginia and Georgia gave up over one billion dollars worth of revenue due to tax breaks. That is money "that is being handed back to the industry rather than going into public funds that could pay for infrastructure, schools, or healthcare." The Negative also highlights that nearby local businesses are benefited by hyper scale data centers, but this does not recognize the hidden costs that the centers have. The water and energy demands of these centers is ginormous. Our card also proves estimates show that in the near-future, the electricity required for data centers will sit around 10 to 15 percent of nationwide electricity demand! One specific example is in Saline Township, Michigan, where a data center is planned to be built and use 1.4 gigawatts of energy, the equivalent to one million households. For individuals, electricity bills may increase by double or more. This is not something you can simply pay for: hyper scale data centers need to use this much electricity to function. The Negative also says that high water use can be solved with recycled water, but our card, Friends of the Earth 26, states that most water is lost during cooling, and although the small remaining amount can be recycled a few times, this process leaves behind salt, bacteria, and mineral deposits that cause clogs in the systems. Hence, data centers still need to continually add more and more freshwater. On the next contention of climate: companies cannot simply expand into other countries. Our card, Roach and Lee 26 from CNBC, details how opposition of data centers in other countries is largely increasing: "More than 70 data center projects in Europe were rejected or restricted between January and April — more than in all of 2025". Similar resistance is showing up in Asia, with South Korean officials announcing that majority consent from residents near planned data center construction would be required to build data centers, and residents have been repeatedly showing disdain against them. Still, even if the companies somehow manage to expand overseas, the data centers are still subject to US governing. The Clarifying Lawful Overseas Use of Data (CLOUD) Act states that US law enforcement agencies can compel US-based technology and cloud based service providers to turn over data in their possession, custody, or control, regardless of whether this data is stored inside or outside the borders of the United States. Furthermore, the Patriot Act that was enacted in response to 9/11 states that the US government has access to any data stored within the US or by companies that operate in the US. Judge, the Negative has conceded that electricity bill increases and huge water use caused by hyperscale data centers are real issues. However, these are not just local issues that can be solved locally. Data center usage is high and increasing. The electricity use is approaching globally concerning levels, and water usage is growing and growing. Their suggested "local solutions" that the status quo can do are idealistic, exaggerated, and cannot solve these problems. Their suggested problems with enacting a moratorium aren't realistic issues. These hyperscale datacenters are a drastic issue, and drastic issues call for drastic measures. The moratorium will put a safeguard on this growth to stop all of these issues and figure out a way to solve them before it is too late. The judge must vote affirmative.

Model answer

Thank you, judge. I’ll go straight down the Con case and then weigh. On their first contention, local economies: first, the tax money is smaller than they claim. Their Harmon card talks about what “a single hyperscale campus can pay,” but many counties give data centers large tax abatements to win the project, so much of that equipment tax is never collected. A best-case example from one county is not a promise for every county. Second, the jobs are temporary. Their own case says fifteen hundred workers “over two years.” After construction, a campus the size of ten football fields runs with a small permanent staff, so the boom ends when the building is finished. And on their small-business card: sales rose during construction. That is exactly the temporary part. A pause delays that spending; it does not erase it. Now their pre-empt. Con concedes that higher electricity bills and heavy water use are “real concerns.” Their only answer is that states are “already writing” rules, but they read no evidence that any rule exists, is enforced, or has lowered a single bill. Without that, their solution is a hope, not a plan. A national pause is the only option on the table that guarantees the problem stops growing while those rules are written. On their second contention, climate and leakage: the warrant is that companies will simply build overseas. But data centers are built close to their customers because data slows down when it travels far, and most of the demand here comes from American customers. Companies also face growing local opposition abroad, so “just move to another country” is not as easy as Con says. Even if some projects do leave, the U.S. would still be able to require clean energy for every center that is built here once the pause ends — the moratorium is how we get those rules in place first. And look closely at their Okafor card. It says about sixty percent of new power contracts were for wind or solar. That means roughly forty percent were not, and because every new campus adds to total demand, utilities are keeping older gas and coal plants running longer just to keep up. The clean share does not erase the extra pollution; it only slows it. A pause gives regulators time to make clean power a condition of building instead of a choice each company makes on its own. On security: they never explain why a pause on new construction moves existing American data overseas. The data in today’s centers stays where it is. Finally, notice what Con never answered: they gave no evidence that local rules can stop bills from rising for families who do not live anywhere near a data center but share the same regional grid. That is why this is a national problem that needs a national pause. So weigh the round this way. Con says their harms come sooner. But our harms are already happening: bills and water use rise the moment a new campus connects to the grid, and they last for the twenty-plus years that campus runs, while their job losses last only as long as the pause. Their harms are temporary and reversible; ours are long-term and get harder to fix with every new campus. That is why we are proud to affirm.
