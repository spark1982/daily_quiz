Event Situation

You are to assume the role of marketing coordinator at Cedar Valley Credit Union, a member-owned financial institution with six branches in and around Des Moines, Iowa.

Next spring the credit union will launch First Paycheck, a checking-and-savings package for members ages 16–19 who are starting their first part-time jobs. It includes a debit card, no monthly fee, automatic transfers into savings, and a budgeting feature in the mobile app. The marketing budget for the launch is $18,000. Last year, a similar youth account was advertised mostly with newspaper ads and brochures in the branch lobbies, and only 140 teens opened accounts — far below the goal of 500. A survey of local high school students found that most of them rarely read newspapers, visit a branch less than once a year, and get information about money from short videos, friends, and family.

The vice president of marketing (judge) has asked you to explain who the target market for First Paycheck is, recommend which advertising media the credit union should use to reach that market, and explain how the different promotional activities should work together during the launch.

The vice president of marketing (judge) will begin the role-play by greeting you and asking to hear your ideas. After you have presented your plan and answered the vice president’s question, the vice president will conclude the role-play by thanking you for your work.

Judge’s follow-up question

If our budget were cut in half and you could keep only one of the media you recommended, which one would you keep, and why?

Target marketing is essentially breaking a market into groups that have certain similarities. Unfortunately, these methods are somewhat archaic and do not apply very well to the younger generation scarcely use newspapers regularly and rarely visit branch lobbies anymore. To ensure that are package is more successful than this one, it is essential that we coordinate the elements in our promotion mix. If we put too much emphasis on types of advertisement, like print newspapers, that rarely reach our target market, newly employed teenagers, than our promotion mix will become less effective. I suggest that we put a much larger stress on what teenagers actually come into contact with regularly. I suggest that we put the most emphasis on social media. That is likely going to be our strongest method of advertisement. The vast majority of teenagers in our target market have some form of social media, like YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, and more, that they interact with daily. Short-form videos or posts that highlight why these teenagers should care about our package can be quite effective. If we ensure that our content is concise and gets straight to the point, then it can appeal to teenagers interested in or already involved in an early job. By contacting large, established companies that teenagers typically gravitate to like fast food chains or other easily accessible establishments, we access an even more direct passage to them. If the companies themselves also promote our package to their employees and potential applicants, it could gravitate more teenagers to both them and us. In summary, to reach as much of our target market, newly employed or soon-to-be employed teenagers, as possible, we can both get involved with social media and work hand-in-hand directly with the companies that employ our target market themselves. What to improve

  • Keep segmentation and target marketing separate. You wrote “This ‘target marketing’ is quite similar market segmentation.” Say instead: “Segmentation divides the market into groups; target marketing chooses one group and builds the plan around it.” Two PIs, two clear explanations.
  • Show the coordination, don’t just say it. Add a short “how it fits together” part: “Every video and employer poster will use the same First Paycheck name and message; videos build awareness in March, employer events turn it into sign-ups in April.”

  • Trim repetition. “Target market” appears about a dozen times. Fewer repeats leaves room for the coordination and budget points above while staying near 400–500 words.

Model answer

Good afternoon, and thank you for meeting with me. Let me start with who First Paycheck is for. Market segmentation means dividing all of our possible members into groups that share traits. For this launch I used three bases: demographic (ages 16 to 19), behavioral (they are opening their first account because they just got their first paycheck), and geographic (students near our six Des Moines branches). Target marketing is the next step: we choose that one segment and design everything — the product, the message, and the media — around it. That choice explains why last year’s campaign reached only 140 teens. Newspapers and lobby brochures are print and out-of-home media, and our survey says these teens rarely read newspapers and visit a branch less than once a year. The message never reached them. So I recommend three media. First, short-form social video on TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube, about $9,000, showing a teen setting up automatic savings in under a minute. Second, word of mouth: a $25 bonus for each member and each friend they refer, about $5,000, because the survey says teens learn about money from friends and family. Third, partnerships with the restaurants and stores that hire teens, about $4,000 for posters and sign-up events in break rooms, so we reach them the week they are hired. These pieces only work if they are coordinated. Every video, poster, and referral card will use the same “First Paycheck” name and the same promise — no monthly fee and savings on autopilot — so a teen who sees it three times recognizes it. The timing builds: videos start in March to create awareness, employer events in April turn interest into sign-ups, and the referral bonus runs all spring to keep it growing. When teens do come in, branch staff will greet them with the same message, so advertising, sales promotion, and personal selling all push one decision. With this plan, I believe we can reach our goal of 500 new accounts.

329 words. Possible answer to the judge’s question: “I would keep social video, because it reaches the most teens per dollar — and I would keep the employer partnerships too, since posters and break-room events cost very little.”
